CFPB's Annual Report of FCRA 611(e) describes the credit-reporting dispute landscape in stark terms. Between January and September 2021, the CFPB received more than 500,000 complaints about credit reporting; "Incorrect information on your report" has been the top issue three years running, alongside "Problem with a credit reporting company's investigation into an existing problem." NCLC's "Disputing Errors in a Credit Report" guide and the CFPB's Circular 2022-07 on reasonable investigations describe the structural failure: credit reporting agencies often conduct what amounts to token investigations, converting consumer disputes into two- or three-digit codes and forwarding them to the furnisher without independent verification.

The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA, 15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.) is the controlling federal statute. It establishes the consumer's right to dispute, the timeframes for response (typically 30 days, extended to 45 in some cases), and the obligation of credit reporting agencies and furnishers to conduct reasonable investigations. CFPB enforcement actions have established that "reasonable" requires substantive engagement with the consumer's submitted documentation, not pattern-matching against template responses.

The user is the wronged consumer. The error may be a debt incorrectly attributed (often after identity theft), a paid debt still reporting as unpaid, a confused-identity issue (relatives with similar names), or a furnisher dispute (a creditor reporting incorrectly). The consumer typically discovers the error at the moment of needing the report — denied for an apartment, a job, a car loan, an insurance rate. The dispute process unfolds over 30–90 days; consumer-advocate analyses document a meaningful share of disputes that result in the error being temporarily removed and then reappearing on the report later from a different furnisher.

(Inferred from CFPB complaint data and NCLC advocacy reporting) the dispute process is structurally adversarial against the consumer despite legal language to the contrary. The three major bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) each have their own dispute interfaces; furnishers vary in cooperation; the CFPB complaint process is a parallel track that sometimes produces results the bureau dispute process did not.
