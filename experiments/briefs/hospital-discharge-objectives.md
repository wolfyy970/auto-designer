The design should make the next action visible at the moment the patient or caregiver picks up the artifact — what to take now, what to watch for today, who to call if a specific symptom appears. The current-moment cue is what's surfaced, not the whole 30-day plan; the rest is reachable but not foregrounded. The failure mode to avoid: surfacing so little that the user cannot find the rest of the plan when they want it, or so much current-moment urgency that the experience reads as a clinical dashboard the patient must monitor.

The design should treat the family caregiver as a peer audience rather than a downstream recipient of the patient's information. Whatever the patient receives, the caregiver can receive in a form that fits the role they are actually performing — medication administration, symptom observation, escalation calls — without requiring them to inherit it secondhand. The failure mode to avoid: routing everything through the patient by default and producing a caregiver experience that is partial, lagged, or dependent on the patient remembering to share.

The design should let the patient or caregiver tell the difference between a symptom that warrants a phone call to the clinic, one that warrants the ED, and one that does not require action. The pattern is recognition-grade — the user is matching what they see against a small set of named conditions, not running a diagnostic flowchart. The failure mode to avoid: a triage experience confident enough to suppress real warning signs, or hedged enough to send every twinge to the ED.

The design should connect the home-side experience to the outpatient clinician who will see the patient at follow-up, in a form the clinician can absorb in the time they have. Questions the patient or caregiver accumulated, medications they actually took, symptoms they noticed — these should reach the next clinical encounter. The failure mode to avoid: a polished patient-facing experience that the receiving clinician cannot ingest, or one that produces so much data the next encounter is buried in it.

The design should fit inside the discharge moment the hospital actually runs, not a redesigned version of that moment. The nurse's involvement is bounded by the minutes available between admissions; whatever the design asks of the discharge conversation has to survive that constraint. The failure mode to avoid: producing a design that demands intentional time the workflow doesn't have, so the result is never actually shipped to patients.
