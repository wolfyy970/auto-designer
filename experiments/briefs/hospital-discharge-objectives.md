The design must enable the patient (or caregiver) to act correctly on their care plan during the first 30 days at home. Acting correctly means: taking the right medication at the right time, recognizing warning signs that warrant a call to the doctor versus a return to the ED, attending scheduled follow-up appointments, completing prescribed activities (PT exercises, dietary changes, wound care). The failure mode to avoid: an experience that feels supportive but does not actually change adherence or recognition — patients leave with the comfort of an app and still misread early heart-failure decompensation.

The design must reach both the patient and the family caregiver. The caregiver is the operational user for many discharge plans (medication administration, transportation, observation, escalation), and they are typically excluded from the discharge conversation by default. The failure mode to avoid: a design that nominally includes the caregiver but in practice still routes through the patient — the caregiver never gets the information at the moment they need it.

The design must respect the patient's stated values and capacities, not just the clinical plan. Older adults often have priorities the discharge plan ignores: living independently, avoiding rehospitalization, maintaining specific aspects of daily life, decisions already documented in advance directives or POLST forms. The failure mode to avoid: optimizing for readmission-reduction at the cost of patient-stated goals, producing technically-successful outcomes that the patient experiences as paternalistic.

The design must close the loop with the outpatient clinician — primary care, specialist, home health — within a clinically appropriate window. Research shows that early outpatient follow-up reduces readmissions, but only when the outpatient clinician actually has the discharge summary, the medication reconciliation, and the patient's questions. The failure mode to avoid: producing a great patient-facing experience that doesn't bridge to the system on the other side.

The design must operate inside real hospital workflows. Discharge happens when the bed is needed for the next admission, often in fewer minutes than the plan deserves. The failure mode to avoid: a design that demands the nurse spend 45 minutes of intentional time on discharge when the day's reality is 15 minutes between patients — producing perfect plans nobody ships.
