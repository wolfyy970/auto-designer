Applicants for SNAP and similar federal safety-net programs are typically navigating multiple simultaneous stressors: recent job loss, reduced hours, illness or caregiving demands, housing instability, or a transition into adulthood after foster care or carceral release. Code for America's analysis of more than 75 state applications across five core federal safety-net programs found that the applications themselves were designed to operate offline and require multiple touchpoints — in person or by mail — with long, arduous enrollment and formal, intimidating communication. The result: more than one in four people living in poverty is not connected to any federally-funded benefit they may be eligible for, and the gap is widest for the people who most need the help.

User research conducted by Code for America and partner counties surfaces consistent applicant experiences. People describe needing a desktop computer to apply during a period when their phone is their only device. They describe library closures on days they could visit. They describe ambiguous questions whose answers depend on context the form does not allow them to explain. They describe submitting and then receiving no confirmation — no way to know if the application is being processed, lost, or rejected. Code for America's published case studies document specific applicant trajectories — for instance, a single mother (Lena) who needed library-computer access she lost on closure days, hit branching questions she couldn't ask about, and after submitting heard nothing back, so could not tell whether to wait, refile, or give up. (Inferred from these consistent patterns) The application experience itself, not eligibility, is the structural barrier for a significant fraction of eligible non-applicants.

The audience is broader than a single demographic. Single parents with variable schedules, immigrants navigating language barriers, older adults losing fixed income, people leaving incarceration, and people recently disabled all apply. Many applicants are first-time applicants who have never used the safety net before; some are returning after years of disconnection. Their digital literacy varies; their access to documentation (pay stubs, lease, SSN cards) varies; their trust in government varies, especially among immigrant and previously-incarcerated populations.

The current state has been documented in case studies like GetCalFresh — a Code for America "digital assistant" rolled out statewide in California that helps applicants navigate SNAP submission. By 2021, three million people had accessed food benefits through it. The pattern is repeatable across other programs and states; it is not yet ubiquitous. Where it exists, it does not replace the underlying state system but rather sits on top of it as a humane intake layer.

The brief's strategic question is whether the right design move is more layers like GetCalFresh, a fundamentally different intake architecture (SMS, voice, human-assistant), or a different theory entirely. Research consistently points toward: applicant trust matters more than application speed; clarity about what happens next matters as much as submission; and the post-submission silence is one of the most damaging parts of the current experience.
