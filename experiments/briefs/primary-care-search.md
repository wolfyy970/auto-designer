# Finding a primary care doctor after relocating — design exploration

A person has moved to a new city. They need to find a primary care doctor. KFF Health News, citing a recent JAMA Internal Medicine report, notes that the number of Americans who can't find a primary care doctor has grown by roughly 20 percent in the past decade; the national primary-care physician shortage was estimated at 17,637 in 2023 and is projected to worsen. The result on the ground: people call a long list of practices only to be told no one is taking new patients, and frustrated patients increasingly turn to online forums asking for leads.

The user has insurance (employer-sponsored, Medicare, or marketplace) and a list of "in-network" doctors that runs to several hundred names. The list is unfiltered — it does not say which doctors accept new patients, which have appointments within the next three months, whether they speak the user's language, whether their office is accessible, or whether their patients tend to feel respected. The user calls one office, navigates a phone tree, holds for ten minutes, and is told the next opening is in five months — or that the doctor is no longer accepting new patients. Multiply by ten or twenty calls. People who already have a health condition (chronic pain, diabetes, anxiety) need to start care; people who are just establishing care give up and only re-engage when something goes wrong.

We are exploring what finding a primary care doctor could be when designed for the patient instead of the directory. The scope is from "I need a new doctor" through "I have an appointment booked." The shape of the solution is open: a patient-side search and booking tool, a phone-first concierge that does the calling, a network/list curated by other patients, a service-design layer over insurer directories, or something else. What we want are distinct strategic directions — not a better doctor finder, but a different theory of what "finding a doctor" means.
