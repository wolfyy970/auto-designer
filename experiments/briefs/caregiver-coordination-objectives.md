The design should hold a single working picture of the parent's care — the current medications and the schedule they are actually on, the upcoming appointments, the open insurance and financial threads, the unresolved questions — visible to the caregiver at the moment they need to act on any of it. The caregiver does not have to reconstruct the picture from email and portal screenshots before they can do the thing in front of them. The failure mode to avoid: a comprehensive dashboard that becomes its own job to maintain, where the caregiver's accuracy of input is more important than the parent's actual care.

The design should make the cost of coordination legible and bounded for the caregiver. Whatever the caregiver does inside the experience — checking on the aide's notes, replying to a sibling, updating a medication, scheduling a follow-up — should take a defined amount of attention and end. The failure mode to avoid: a notification surface that fills the caregiver's day with small interruptions, or a design that responds to caregiver overload by hiding work that still needs doing.

The design should let the family operate as a multi-actor system without forcing equal participation or scoreboard transparency. Siblings, spouses, partners, in-home aides, and the parent themselves participate at different levels of involvement and trust; the design accommodates that distribution. The failure mode to avoid: a contribution-tracking surface that turns who-is-doing-what into a comparison that poisons the family relationship the caregiver is trying to preserve.

The design should hold the parent as a person whose autonomy and dignity are part of the care plan, not as an object the caregiver manages. Where the parent participates, the experience reads as theirs; where capacity changes over time, the design accommodates the transition without forcing a hard "switch to caregiver-controlled" moment. The failure mode to avoid: a caregiver-side picture so complete that the parent disappears as a participant, or a parent-side surface so deferential that real cognitive changes are not addressed.

The design should connect to the formal care system at the points where it actually helps — pulling forward information the caregiver would otherwise have to reconstruct, surfacing the question the next clinician is going to ask. The failure mode to avoid: an isolated caregiver-side surface that re-renders information the medical system already holds, or one that promises integration and produces stale, partial reads of clinical data the caregiver cannot trust to act on.
