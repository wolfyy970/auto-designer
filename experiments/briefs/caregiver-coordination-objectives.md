The design must reduce coordination errors that have direct consequences for the care recipient: missed medications, missed appointments, duplicated services, preventable ED visits. The failure mode to avoid: an experience that feels supportive but doesn't change the error rate.

The design must reduce caregiver burden. The failure mode to avoid: adding to the caregiver's work in the name of supporting them.

The design must coordinate within the family — sibling involvement that ranges from supportive to fractious — without amplifying conflict. The failure mode to avoid: a tool that becomes the locus of family disputes about who's doing what.

The design must respect the care recipient's autonomy and dignity. The failure mode to avoid: a caregiver-side experience that treats the aging parent as the object rather than the person.

The design must bridge to the formal care system (PCP, specialists, home-health, hospital, insurance) where possible. The failure mode to avoid: an isolated caregiver-side experience that doesn't connect to the systems whose information matters most.
