US employment law applies to documented performance language. Documented patterns that imply age discrimination (ADEA), race or sex discrimination (Title VII), disability discrimination (ADA), or retaliation create legal exposure. The design must not produce language that violates these — but also must not over-correct into vagueness that violates the company's own performance-management commitments.

Company policy and the specific performance-management framework (OKRs, 9-box, MBOs, ratings vs ratings-less) constrain the design. The design must hold the framework correctly; producing language that doesn't fit the framework wastes the manager's time.

Employee notes and review drafts are sensitive. Architectures that store this data must respect confidentiality; cross-organization data exposure has real harm.

AI assistance must be transparent and bias-checked. AI-generated language that introduces bias (gendered descriptions, ageist phrasings) at scale is a documented risk. The design must surface AI assistance as assistance, not as authority, and the manager must verify.

Accessibility (WCAG 2.2 AA) applies. Audience includes managers with disabilities; the experience must work with assistive technology.

The architecture must hold the dual relationship: the manager is the user, but the employee is the subject. Employee-favorable defaults (the employee can see their review, can respond, can request corrections) must be designed in.
