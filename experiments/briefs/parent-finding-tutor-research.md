Brookings Institution research on tutoring effectiveness (notably the "Tutoring Corps" proposal and the "Counterintuitive strategies for overcoming pandemic learning loss with high-dosage tutoring" piece) establishes a clear evidence base: high-dosage tutoring (HDT) — personalized, 1:1 or 2:1 student-to-tutor ratio, multiple times per week, ideally during the school day — produces large learning gains for low-performing students. Less-intensive tutoring (once-a-week sessions outside school) under-performs many other educational interventions. Hechinger Report coverage of the same evidence base reaches the same conclusions.

The consumer tutoring market is largely structured against the evidence. Most options available to parents — local chains (Mathnasium, Kumon, Sylvan), online services (Wyzant, Varsity Tutors), peer-tutored platforms (Schoolhouse.world), AI-tutor apps — operate at lower intensities and at student-to-tutor ratios farther from 1:1 than the evidence supports. Cost ranges from free (Khan Academy, Schoolhouse.world peer-tutoring) to substantial (private 1:1 tutors at $50–$150/hour). School-based intervention programs (Title I tutoring, special education services, MTSS Tier 2 and 3) are the closest to evidence-based intervention but are gate-kept by referral, enrollment, and capacity.

The user is the parent. They are typically not the math expert; they may be a math expert and still face the parent-vs-teacher dynamics with their own child. They are working with limited time and bounded budget. They are caring about the child's emotional state (a struggling 6th-grader may resent the framing "you need a tutor"; the child may be acting out at school in ways that compound the academic issue). They have access to information — the school's recommendations, the child's teacher, the child's grades, online reviews of tutoring services — but no clear way to map options to evidence.

(Inferred from the gap between Brookings' evidence base and the consumer tutoring market) the parent is navigating a market that is not aligned to what works. The design problem includes consumer-education (what works) and matching (find a fit that's evidence-aligned within the parent's constraints).
