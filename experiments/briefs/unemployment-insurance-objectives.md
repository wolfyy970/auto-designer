The design should let the claimant answer each application question with the information they actually have in front of them — pay stubs they may or may not have kept, job dates they may have to reconstruct from memory, employer addresses they may not remember exactly. The design move is to surface, at each question, what counts as a valid answer and what to do when the answer isn't reachable, instead of presenting questions as if the right answer is always at hand. The failure mode to avoid: an application so accommodating of uncertainty that the resulting submission cannot be processed by the eligibility worker on the other side, creating a downstream delay that defeats the upstream forgiveness.

The design should surface claim status in language the claimant can use to plan, naming what the status actually means for whether and when money arrives. The design move is to make the system's current state visible at the level of "what should I do next" — pending what, blocked by what, scheduled for what — rather than at the level of internal workflow codes. The failure mode to avoid: a friendly-looking status that hides a stalled file, where the polish of the surface defeats the trust that comes from honest reporting of bad news.

The design should reach the audiences the current system fails — variable-schedule and gig workers whose work histories don't fit the standard forms, claimants with limited English proficiency, claimants without a stable home address, formerly-incarcerated claimants navigating reentry, claimants whose only device is a phone with intermittent connectivity. The design move is to let these realities be answerable inside the flow rather than requiring the claimant to abandon the path. The failure mode to avoid: a redesigned flow that polishes the median experience while leaving the same populations excluded.

The design should respect the eligibility worker on the agency side as a user of the system whose load determines how fast every claim is decided. The design move is to keep what the claimant submits in the shape the worker needs to act on, with the right supporting documentation captured inside the flow rather than chased after. The failure mode to avoid: a claimant-side experience that creates downstream rework for civil servants, slowing decisions for every claimant whose file is queued behind it.

The design should hold identity verification without making honest claimants feel like suspects. The design move is to keep the fraud-control questions and document checks adjacent to the application without making them the dominant note of the experience. The failure mode to avoid: identity proofing patterns that fail asymmetrically against the populations that most need the benefit — older adults whose credit history is thin, claimants whose ID documents don't match standard databases, claimants whose name has changed.
