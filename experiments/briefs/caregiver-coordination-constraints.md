HIPAA applies to the care recipient's PHI. The caregiver typically does not have legal authority over the care recipient's data without specific authorization (HIPAA-authorization forms, healthcare proxy, durable power of attorney). The design must navigate this correctly.

Financial-decision authority is similarly regulated. POA documentation, joint accounts, and bank-specific authorization processes vary; the design must not assume authority the caregiver does not have.

State elder-law and adult-protective-services rules vary. The architecture must hold this correctly, particularly in cases where the care recipient's cognitive status is changing.

The care recipient may have dementia or other conditions that change decisional capacity over time. The design must handle the transition from "parent makes decisions, caregiver assists" to "caregiver makes decisions on parent's behalf" with respect for the legal and ethical specifics.

Equity-of-effort tracking among siblings is a delicate design space. Tools that count tasks (who did how many appointments) can produce useful transparency or can poison family relationships; the design must be deliberate.

Accessibility (WCAG 2.2 AA) is a floor. The audience includes caregivers with their own disabilities; the experience must work with assistive technology.

The architecture must work across distance. Many caregivers live in a different city or state than the parent; the design cannot assume co-located access to information or paperwork.
