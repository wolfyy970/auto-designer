KFF Health News reporting, citing JAMA Internal Medicine analysis, documents that the number of Americans who cannot find a primary care doctor has grown by approximately 20 percent in the past decade. The Health Resources and Services Administration estimated a national primary-care physician shortage of 17,637 in 2023, with projections suggesting it will worsen through this decade. Patients describe calling a long list of in-network primary care offices only to be told the practice is not accepting new patients or has appointments months out; the absence of accurate, current acceptance data is one of the most-cited friction points.

AHRQ's National Center for Excellence in Primary Care Research has documented that strong primary care is associated with better population health, fewer ED visits, and lower total cost of care — and that access to primary care (defined as the ability to establish a relationship with a clinician one can see for both acute and ongoing needs) is the prerequisite to all those outcomes. The Commonwealth Fund's Kaiser/Commonwealth survey of primary-care providers documents the supply-side picture: physicians retiring, fewer trainees choosing primary care, increased administrative load reducing capacity for new patients.

The user is a person who has moved (the most common trigger to need a new PCP) or whose previous doctor has retired, dropped their insurance, or otherwise become unavailable. They have insurance — employer-sponsored, ACA marketplace, Medicare, Medicaid, or other — and an in-network directory that runs from dozens to hundreds of names. The directory often lacks the information they most need: is this practice accepting new patients today, what's the next appointment, does the doctor speak my language, is the office accessible, does this practice handle my health needs (chronic-disease management, women's health, geriatric care, etc.).

(Inferred from documented patterns of patients giving up after multiple rejections and only re-engaging when something goes wrong) the abandonment of the search is itself a public-health problem. KFF Health News also documents a related phenomenon: people who haven't seen their doctor in a few years may need to find a new one when the practice updates rosters or drops patients who haven't been seen recently.
