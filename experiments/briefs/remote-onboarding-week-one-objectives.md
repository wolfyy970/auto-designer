The design must accelerate the new hire to their first meaningful contribution — a real piece of work shipped, a question answered for a teammate, a contribution to a project conversation. The failure mode to avoid: a "completed onboarding tasks" metric that registers high while the new hire is still functionally observing, not participating.

The design must build the new hire's social map — who works on what, who to ask for help in what domains, who is the person to know about the codebase / customer / culture / process. The failure mode to avoid: introducing the new hire to "the team" as a single block during orientation, leaving them with names and faces but no working understanding of who-knows-what.

The design must protect the new hire's cognitive load during the first three days, where the volume of new information (people, tools, vocabulary, context) is already at capacity. The failure mode to avoid: optimizing for the manager's or HR's checklist completion, which produces a calendar so full the new hire has no time to read, think, or absorb.

The design must close the loop with the manager — surfacing what's been learned, what's confusing, where the new hire feels stuck — without making the new hire feel surveilled or graded. The failure mode to avoid: a manager-facing dashboard that the new hire knows about and performs for, eroding the honesty needed for the manager to actually help.

The design must work across the variety of remote/hybrid configurations companies have (fully remote, hub-and-spoke, occasionally in person), without assuming the new hire will visit an office in the first week.
