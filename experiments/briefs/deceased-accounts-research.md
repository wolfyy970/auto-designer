The personal representative — typically a spouse, adult child, or sibling designated as executor or administrator — inherits an administrative ordeal at one of the most cognitively-impaired moments of their life. CFPB consumer guidance documents the process: identifying every account the deceased held, contacting each institution with death certificates and letters of authority, settling debts from estate assets, and distributing remaining property per state law. The work spans dozens of institutions: banks, brokerages, credit cards, mortgages, utilities, employer retirement accounts, life insurance, Social Security, the deceased's digital life (email, photos, social media, subscription services), and any open legal or tax matters.

Debt collectors begin contacting survivors within days of the obituary. The CFPB documents that survivors are commonly misled into believing they are personally responsible for the deceased's debts; in most cases they are not, unless they were a co-signer, joint account holder, or live in a community-property state for certain debts. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) limits what collectors may say to survivors, but limits are often ignored in practice. (Inferred from CFPB complaint patterns) the asymmetry of information between collectors and grieving survivors is the most damaging part of the early-weeks experience.

The audience is not a single demographic. Adult children losing aging parents predominate, but the experience also lands on young widows and widowers, on adult siblings of an unmarried decedent, on grandchildren or partners in non-traditional family structures. They have varying relationships to the deceased's finances — some were already financially intertwined and know the accounts; many are starting from a sealed mailbox and a key to an apartment they have rarely visited. Most have never been an executor before; most will not be again.

The current state is a patchwork of resources. The CFPB's "Managing Someone Else's Money" series, attorney-published probate guides, the funeral home's grief-resources packet, online checklists from financial publishers, and state court self-help pages all provide accurate information — at the cost of being encyclopedic. They list everything the executor might need; none knows what the user needs today. (Inferred from caregiver-research patterns and the consistency of "I didn't know where to start" reports) the gap is not information availability but information sequencing tailored to this estate's specifics.
