The design should surface, from the tenant's facts, the specific defenses and procedural arguments that apply to their case — improper notice, habitability, rent claim errors, retaliation, unlawful self-help — rather than presenting the full universe of possibilities and leaving the tenant to pattern-match. The failure mode to avoid: dumping the encyclopedia of possible defenses on someone who needs to know which two or three apply to them and walking away with more uncertainty than they arrived with.

The design should distinguish between cases where a defense exists and cases where the tenant's best move is negotiation, settlement, or filing an answer to preserve options — and reflect that distinction in what it tells the tenant. The failure mode to avoid: producing false optimism for tenants without a defense, delaying them from the negotiation or relocation work that would actually serve them while the court date approaches.

The design should make the tenant's documentation legible as evidence — text messages about repair requests, photos of conditions, rent receipts, lease copies — so the tenant recognizes what they already have rather than feeling like they need things they don't. The failure mode to avoid: an evidence frame so demanding that the tenant decides they have nothing useful and shows up to court empty-handed when their phone gallery actually contained the relevant proof.

The design should produce court-ready paperwork (answer, discovery requests, motions where applicable) in the format the tenant's specific court accepts, with the filing path explicit. The failure mode to avoid: generating legally-sound text that never gets filed because the tenant did not know which window to walk it to or which fee to ask to have waived.

The design should pace the work to the tenant's life — short sessions reachable late at night between work and childcare, resumable across days, surviving the interruption of a returning landlord call mid-flow. The failure mode to avoid: a long-form intake that drives the tenant away on session one before they have anything usable, leaving them with the original stack of papers and no plan.

The design should make its boundary with attorneys and legal-aid clinics visible — when to keep going on their own, when to call the clinic, when the case is over the design's head — rather than positioning itself as a substitute for representation. The failure mode to avoid: serving every tenant the same way and producing a parallel system that pulls demand away from over-capacity legal-aid clinics while leaving the hardest cases under-served.
