# Filing for unemployment insurance — design exploration

A person who has just lost their job — through a layoff, a closure, hours cut, or a contract ending — files for unemployment insurance through their state's labor agency. The application is the gate to benefits the worker is entitled to under both state and federal law. The Nava team's research with California's Employment Development Department during the COVID-19 spike documented what applicants actually face: 88 percent of research participants didn't understand what a required piece of information called an "EDDCAN" was or where to find it; people working low-income or variable schedule jobs have a harder time filing for claims; claimants report not knowing what their claim status is, what they owe in next steps, or whether they'll receive a payment.

The user is recently unemployed. They may have run through their savings or be close to it. They are filing this application on a phone, in line at a job center, between caregiving shifts, late at night. They are also filling out an application designed by lawyers, layered with anti-fraud questions written in legalese, asking for information from pay stubs they may not have access to. If they get a single answer wrong, the claim is delayed or denied, and they have to start over or appeal. Once they file, the status communication is opaque: a paper letter weeks later, or a portal login they have to remember the password for.

We are exploring what filing for unemployment could feel like when designed for the claimant's lived experience. The scope is the filing flow plus the first 30 days of claim status communication. The shape of the solution is open: a redesigned web flow, a phone-first intake assistant, an SMS-based status companion, a paper-form-companion, or something else. What we want are distinct strategic directions for what a humane unemployment application could meaningfully be.
