# A parent finding tutoring for a kid struggling in math — design exploration

A parent has a child in 4th, 6th, or 8th grade who is struggling in math. The child's grades have slipped; the teacher has sent a note; the standardized test results came back lower than expected. Brookings and Hechinger research is unambiguous on what works: high-dosage tutoring (HDT) — defined as personalized, with a 1:1 or 2:1 student-to-tutor ratio, occurring multiple times per week, during the school day where possible — produces large learning gains for low-performing students. Less-intensive tutoring (once-a-week sessions) under-performs many other educational interventions. Most of what's available to parents on the open market does not match what works.

The user is the parent. They want help for their kid and they have a few hours over the next two weeks to set it up. They are facing a tangle of options: the local tutoring chain (predictable but expensive, 1:4 or 1:6 ratios, weekly), a private tutor matched through a friend (varying quality, varying availability), an online tutoring service (Wyzant, Varsity Tutors, Khan Academy + Schoolhouse.world, etc.), the school's own intervention program (if eligible), or AI-tutor apps that promise personalization. The parent cannot easily tell which option matches the research evidence and which is marketing. They are not the math expert; the child is sensitive to the framing ("you need a tutor" can feel like a judgment); the cost question is real.

We are exploring what finding the right tutoring could feel like for this parent. The scope is from "we need help" through "we have a plan and a tutor we trust." The shape of the solution is open: an evidence-based tutoring-matching platform, a parent-advisor companion that helps assess what kind of intervention fits, a school-and-home coordination layer, an AI-tutor evaluator surface, or something else. We want strategic directions.
