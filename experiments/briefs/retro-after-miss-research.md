Atlassian's Team Playbook and Scrum.org's published material are the working canon for sprint retrospectives. Atlassian's blog documents a McKinsey finding cited as: teams that regularly hold retrospectives are roughly 14 percent more likely to achieve project goals. Scrum.org's "21 Sprint Retrospective Anti-Patterns" article catalogs the failure modes — chief among them, the absence of psychological safety leading to blame-cycling.

Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety (notably "The Fearless Organization," 2018) provides the foundational research vocabulary. Edmondson defines psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking; her research from Google's Project Aristotle (popularized) and elsewhere connects psychological safety to learning behaviors and team performance. Retros are a stress-test of psychological safety: without it, the retro becomes performance art.

The user is the scrum master, agile coach, or facilitating engineering manager. They have 45–60 minutes booked, a tired team, and the explicit problem of the missed target sitting in the room. Their job is to make a conversation happen that would otherwise not happen. They are often working against the team's defensive patterns (the engineer who blames the spec, the PM who blames the engineers, the designer who feels nothing they say matters), against leadership-in-the-room dynamics (the VP who needs to hear that things are going well), and against retrospective-fatigue from years of retros that didn't change anything.

(Inferred from the McKinsey 14-percent finding and the consistency of anti-pattern documentation) the difference between teams that gain from retros and teams that don't is not the format of the retro but the conditions around it. Existing tools (Miro templates, FunRetro, EasyRetro, Jira retro modules) capture data well; the human work of getting to honesty is mostly outside the tool.
