The 2020 ADA/ADCES Consensus Report on Diabetes Self-management Education and Support (DSMES) — endorsed by the American Diabetes Association, the Association of Diabetes Care & Education Specialists, the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, AAFP, AAPA, AANP, and APhA — is the canonical practice document for what newly-diagnosed Type 2 diabetes patients should receive. Yet only about 6.8 percent of privately insured newly-diagnosed patients participate in DSMES within 12 months, and only about 5 percent of newly-diagnosed Medicare patients do. The gap between consensus practice and actual experience is the design problem.

Peer-reviewed qualitative research (BMC Health Services Research, PLOS One, Current Diabetes Reports, PMC publications) documents what newly-diagnosed patients actually want in the first weeks: immediate emotional support for unexpressed concerns, information about available resources to know when and where to seek help, and a connection with a health-care partner after diagnosis who serves as a guide. The research also catalogs failure modes: patients overwhelmed by all-at-once information loading, patients ashamed and blame-internalizing (they "caused" this), patients receiving conflicting advice across providers, patients without family support whose dietary changes destabilize a household economy.

The user is the newly-diagnosed patient. They are typically in middle adulthood (Type 2 diabetes incidence rises with age, though increasingly diagnosed earlier), often have other concurrent health conditions (hypertension, obesity, sleep apnea), and frequently have a family history they have been watching with anxiety. They are processing a diagnosis that affects diet, exercise, medication, monitoring, work life, and family life simultaneously. (Inferred from the consistency of qualitative reports across studies) the diagnosis lands on a person who is often already managing a lot.

The clinical context is fragmented. The diagnosing physician (typically PCP, sometimes endocrinologist), the pharmacist filling metformin, the nutritionist if one is recommended, the certified diabetes care and education specialist (CDCES) if one is involved, the lab where A1c testing happens — each operates in their own system. The patient is the integrator. Apps in the market range from glucose-logging utilities to coaching platforms (Livongo/Teladoc, Virta for low-carb-focused care, Omada) to peer-support communities; (Inferred) none yet handles the diagnosis-moment guide role consistently.
