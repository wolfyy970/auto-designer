# Applying for food assistance — design exploration

In the United States, federal nutrition programs like SNAP (food stamps) are designed to support people through periods of low income. The application is the gate. Code for America's analysis of more than 75 state applications across five federal safety-net programs found that the applications themselves were built to operate offline and require multiple touchpoints — in-person or by mail — with long and arduous enrollment processes and formal, intimidating communication.

The user is someone who needs food assistance now. They may have lost a job, had hours cut, gotten sick, or be raising a kid alone on inconsistent income. They are not the demographic the application was designed for. People applying tell stories like Lena's, a single mom Code for America documented: she needed a desktop computer to apply, lost access on library closure days, hit confusing questions she had no way to ask about, and received no confirmation after submitting — so she didn't know whether to wait, refile, or give up. Roughly one in four people living in poverty is not connected to any federally-funded benefit they may be eligible for; more than $60 billion in benefits goes unclaimed every year, much of it lost in the application gap.

We are exploring what a SNAP application could be when designed around the applicant rather than the agency. The brief is the application experience itself — from the moment someone realizes they might be eligible to the moment a county worker has what they need to determine eligibility. The shape of the solution is open: it could be a redesigned web flow, a text-message-based intake, a phone-first intake with a human assistant, a paper-form-companion app, or something else. We are not committed to a single surface. What we want from this round are distinct strategic directions for what a humane, accessible food-assistance application could meaningfully be.
