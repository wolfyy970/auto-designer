The design should meet the patient at the emotional reality of diagnosis week — fear, shame, confusion, occasional grief, sometimes relief — and let the experience itself reflect that this is a hard week, not a feature-onboarding flow. The design move is calibrated pacing of what the patient is asked to do, with the first interactions weighted toward acknowledgment and orientation rather than data capture. The failure mode to avoid: a design so careful with the patient's state that it never gets to substance — endless validation without any of the practical scaffolding the diagnosis actually requires.

The design should sequence the changes the patient is now facing so the patient experiences one decision at a time rather than the full slate at once. The design move is staged introduction — metformin first, glucose monitoring next, dietary work as it becomes reachable — visible to the patient so they can see what's coming and what's not yet. The failure mode to avoid: a sequence so prescriptive that it loses fit with the patient's actual life, where one fixed order is imposed on patients whose circumstances would order things differently.

The design should let the patient see and reach the resources their diagnosis entitles them to — DSMES sessions, dietitian referrals, CDCES involvement, often peer programs — without requiring them to discover that these resources exist by reading the consensus document. The design move is making the routing visible at the moment the patient could use it. The failure mode to avoid: a referral surface that pushes every patient toward every resource, where the screen of options becomes its own overwhelm.

The design should treat dietary change as a household reality rather than a single-person behavior change. The design move is content and interactions that make space for the people the patient eats with — partner, children, parents, roommates — to be part of what changes, without making household participation a precondition. The failure mode to avoid: a design that requires household coordination to function, excluding patients who live alone, who eat in shared institutional settings, or whose household will not participate.

The design should hold the patient's clinical care team — diagnosing physician, pharmacy, dietitian, CDCES — as the system of record rather than competing with it. The design move is presenting information in ways that complement what a clinician will say at the next visit, and surfacing when something the patient is seeing in the experience should become a question for the clinician. The failure mode to avoid: a design so deferential to the clinical record that it provides no value between visits, leaving the patient with nothing to lean on in the long stretches between appointments.
