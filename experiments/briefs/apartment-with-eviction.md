# Apartment search for a renter with a past eviction — design exploration

A person with an eviction record is looking for a new place to live. The Eviction Lab at Princeton has documented that an eviction filing — regardless of the case's eventual outcome — functions as a "scarlet E" that follows people for years. Of the 3.6 million eviction court records tracked from 2011 to 2015, more than one in five contained little information on the resolution of the case. Roughly 90 percent of landlords use third-party tenant screening tools that compile rental, credit, employment, income, and criminal histories into a simplified score or recommendation — often devoid of context or accuracy. Even an eviction that was dismissed, settled, or filed in error often shows up the same way as a completed eviction-for-cause.

The user is a renter who has previously been evicted (perhaps after losing a job, a medical event, or a dispute with a prior landlord) and is now ready to find a stable home. They have rebuilt their finances, have a job, may have references, but the screening report is still the screening report. They face blanket screening policies, automated decisions made by software, and landlords who will not even meet them. They spend more time and money on application fees than other renters, often discover the rejection only after submitting, and have no path to contest a screening report they believe is inaccurate.

We are exploring what a tool for this experience could be. The scope is the apartment search journey from "I'm looking" through "I've signed a lease." The shape of the solution is open: a renter-side advocate tool that bundles records and context, a screening-transparency layer between renters and landlords, a network of landlords willing to consider context, a record-relief / sealing facilitator, or something else. The system today is built for landlords; we want strategic directions that center the renter.
