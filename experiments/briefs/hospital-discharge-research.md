Older adults discharging from inpatient care are among the most vulnerable users in any healthcare workflow. AHRQ's body of work on care transitions — Project RED (Re-Engineered Discharge), Project BOOST, and the Care Transitions Intervention literature — has consistently documented the discharge moment as where patient-safety incidents cluster. AHRQ has reported that roughly 14 percent of hospital patients are readmitted within 30 days, with the rate rising with age and acuity (AHRQ, Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project; CMS Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program reporting). (Inferred from the consistency of these findings across decades of AHRQ work) a substantial share of these readmissions trace to the transition itself rather than the underlying illness — patients and caregivers leaving without the information they need in a form they can act on.

The patient's experience during discharge is shaped by physical and cognitive factors that the workflow rarely accommodates. Patients may be in pain, on medications that affect cognition, recovering from anesthesia or sleep-deprived, and emotionally activated by the discharge itself. AHRQ guidance has noted that patients' lack of physical or emotional readiness to learn is a documented barrier; discharge instructions are often unclear and not tailored to individual learning styles or health literacy needs. (Inferred) The discharge moment is not when patients absorb information; it is when they nominally receive it.

The caregiver — typically an adult child or spouse — is often not in the room during the discharge conversation, even when they will administer the care at home. AHRQ's IDEAL Discharge Planning guidance explicitly calls for family-caregiver inclusion as a strategy to reduce readmission, recognizing that the person who will manage medications, watch for warning signs, drive to follow-ups, and identify when to call has a different information need from the patient. AARP's *Caregiving in the US* reporting has documented the prevalence of unpaid family caregivers managing complex post-discharge regimens. The workflow nevertheless remains patient-centered by default rather than patient-and-family-centered.

What the audience confronts today is a paper handout, occasionally accompanied by a tablet-based teach-back, and sometimes a phone-based follow-up program in better-resourced systems. Project RED and the Care Transitions Intervention literature have demonstrated that programs which reconcile medications, instruct patients and caregivers in self-care methods, prepare patient-centered discharge instructions, and facilitate communication with outpatient providers reduce readmissions and ED visits. These programs are effective but staff-intensive; most hospitals do not run them, and most patients still leave with paper.

The audience varies along axes that filter what reaches them. Health literacy varies widely — AHRQ has documented the gap between the literacy level discharge materials are written at and the level the median patient reads at. Home environments vary: Wi-Fi may or may not exist, a co-resident caregiver may or may not be present, transportation to follow-up may or may not be available, and the home itself may or may not be safe for the post-discharge state. Cognitive and sensory capacity in the older-adult population spans a wide range, including patients living with mild cognitive impairment, hearing loss, low vision, and reduced fine-motor dexterity (AHRQ patient-safety literature; AARP older-adult research). The strategic positioning sits in the gap between the discharge conversation as it currently happens and the first follow-up appointment one to two weeks later — the window where most preventable failures occur and where current offerings are sparse.
