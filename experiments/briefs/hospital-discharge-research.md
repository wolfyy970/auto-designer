Older adults discharging from inpatient care are among the most vulnerable users in any healthcare workflow. AHRQ's body of research, including Project RED (Re-Engineered Discharge), Project BOOST (Better Outcomes for Older Adults through Safer Transitions), and the Care Transitions trial, has consistently documented that the discharge transition is the moment patient-safety incidents cluster. Roughly 14 percent of hospital patients are readmitted within 30 days; the rate rises with age and acuity. A meaningful share of readmissions is preventable — driven by transition failures, not the underlying illness.

The patient's experience during discharge is shaped by physical and cognitive factors that the workflow rarely accommodates. Patients may be in pain, on medications that affect cognition, recovering from anesthesia or sleep-deprived, and emotionally activated by the discharge itself (relief, anxiety, fear). AHRQ research notes that patients' lack of physical or emotional readiness to learn is a documented barrier; discharge instructions are often unclear and not tailored to individual learning styles or health literacy needs. (Inferred from the consistency of these findings across decades) the discharge moment is not when patients absorb information; it is when they nominally receive it.

The caregiver — typically an adult child or spouse — is often not in the room during the discharge conversation, even when they will administer the care. AHRQ's IDEAL Discharge Planning guidance explicitly calls for family-caregiver inclusion as a strategy to reduce readmission, recognizing that the person who will manage medications, watch for warning signs, drive to follow-ups, and identify when to call has a different information need than the patient. Yet the workflow is patient-centered by default, not patient-and-family-centered.

The current state is a paper handout, occasionally accompanied by a tablet-based teach-back. Project RED and Care Transitions trials demonstrate that interventions which (a) reconcile medications, (b) instruct patients and caregivers in self-care methods, (c) prepare patient-centered discharge instructions, and (d) facilitate communication with outpatient providers reduce readmissions and ED visits. These programs are effective but expensive — most hospitals do not run them; most patients still leave with paper.

The strategic question is whether the right design move is a better paper handout, a digital companion that follows the patient home, a redesigned conversation experience inside the discharge moment, a caregiver-side companion that takes over once the patient is home, or a service-design intervention that shifts the work earlier (during the inpatient stay) when the patient is calmer.
