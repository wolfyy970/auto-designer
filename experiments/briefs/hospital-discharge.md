# Discharge instructions for older adults going home — design exploration

According to AHRQ, roughly 14% of hospital patients are readmitted within 30 days of discharge, and that number rises significantly with age. A meaningful share of those readmissions are preventable — driven not by the underlying illness but by what happens (or fails to happen) in the transition from inpatient care to home. Patients leave with a stack of paper instructions, a list of new medications, follow-up appointments to make, and warning signs to watch for. The instructions are often unclear and not tailored to individual health literacy needs. The patient is frequently still physically or emotionally unprepared to absorb information at the moment it is handed to them. The caregiver who will actually administer the next 30 days may not be in the room.

The user is an older adult — say, 75 — recovering from a heart-failure exacerbation, a stroke, a fall with hip repair, or pneumonia. The patient is being discharged this afternoon. Their daughter has driven in from two states away to help for the first week. The nurse has 15 minutes to walk through six pages of paperwork before the next admission needs the room. Once the patient gets home, what they remember of those 15 minutes is fragmentary at best. Existing interventions like AHRQ's Project RED and the Care Transitions trial use specially-trained staff to reconcile medications, instruct patients and caregivers, and follow up in the first week — and both have been shown to reduce readmissions. But most hospitals don't run those programs; most patients still leave with paper.

We are exploring what a discharge experience could be when designed for the patient and the family caregiver, in the moment they leave the hospital and the days that follow. The scope is the discharge moment plus the first 7–14 days at home. The shape of the solution is open: it could be a printed companion redesign, a tablet handed to the patient at discharge, an SMS/voice follow-up rhythm with the family caregiver, an EHR-integrated patient-facing companion, or something else. Out of scope: any change to the clinical care plan itself. What we want are distinct strategic directions for what a discharge experience could meaningfully be — real bets, not variants of the existing paper handout.
