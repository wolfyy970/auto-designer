CDC's Yellow Book and Travelers' Health guidance describe the regulatory and practical landscape for travelers carrying prescription medications across borders. Many common US medications — including those for ADHD (stimulants), pain (opioids, some non-opioids), depression (some SSRIs/SNRIs in specific countries), allergy/decongestants (pseudoephedrine), and ADHD-adjacent medications — are restricted or illegal in destination countries; consequences for being caught range from confiscation to denial of entry to arrest. The FDA's personal-importation guidance addresses the inverse problem (bringing medications back into the US).

The practical workflow is multi-step: the patient must confirm their destination's rules (often via embassy or consulate guidance, which is fragmented across foreign-government websites), obtain a "vacation override" from their insurance to fill more than a normal supply, obtain a letter or certificate from the prescriber describing the medication and dosing, package medications in original containers, and carry copies of prescriptions including generic names. The CDC also recommends a 1–2 week extra supply for delays and notes that US prescriptions cannot be filled by overseas pharmacies.

(Inferred from CDC/State Department guidance combined with the structural absence of a single owner of this workflow) the patient is responsible for orchestrating across pharmacy, prescriber, insurance, employer (for HSA/FSA implications), and customs authorities in possibly multiple jurisdictions. No single entity owns the work; the patient is the integrator by default.

The audience is broader than business travelers. Students studying abroad, retirees taking long trips, people with chronic conditions visiting family in another country, people emigrating or returning for an extended visit. Some carry medications that are routine in the US and forbidden where they are going (Adderall in Japan is the canonical example); some carry medications that are routine in the destination and difficult to obtain in transit countries. The first realization that this is hard often comes 10–14 days before departure, when "I should refill my prescription" leads to "wait, will customs let me…"
