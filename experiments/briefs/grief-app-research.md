Users in this product space are navigating grief weeks to months after a meaningful loss — past the acute logistics phase but early enough that grief shapes their daily experience. The losses vary significantly: parent, partner, friend, pregnancy, pet, anticipatory loss from terminal diagnosis. The grief research literature has moved past stage models toward more individualized framings. The Dual Process Model (Stroebe & Schut, 1999) describes oscillation between loss-orientation and restoration-orientation rather than linear progression; Continuing Bonds theory (Klass, Silverman, & Nickman, 1996) describes ongoing relationships with the deceased as healthy rather than as something to "let go." Prolonged Grief Disorder was added to DSM-5-TR (2022) as a recognized condition, distinct from depression and PTSD. (Inferred) Designs that assume linear progression through five stages are working from a model the field has largely moved away from.

The current market offers three rough categories, each with a structural gap for the audience above. Funeral logistics sites (Everdays, Ever Loved, Eternal) serve a narrow pre-funeral window and stay transactional. Peer support communities and subreddits (r/GriefSupport, Modern Loss community, online widow/widower forums) work for some but expose users to unpredictability and judgment. Journaling apps that add a "feeling sad" mood option (Day One, Daylio with sentiment tags) offer no meaningful engagement with grief beyond a single-tap log. (Inferred) The structural gap is the absence of offerings between the transactional window and the social/community window — a space the academic and practitioner literature (Center for Complicated Grief at Columbia University, ADEC) describes as underserved.

Privacy in grief contexts is documented and consequential. Work on bereavement and digital presence (Center for Complicated Grief, ADEC) describes bereaved individuals' frequent desire to keep grief content separate from broader social presentation. Pew Research has documented similar privacy concerns around sensitive personal data more broadly. (Inferred) Grief content surfacing in social feeds, search history, or shared device contexts is a deal-breaker class of privacy violation for this audience — more consequential than for many other sensitive-data domains, because grief is often kept private from the same social network the user otherwise shares with.

The audience varies along axes that filter direction-fit. Pregnancy loss has its own literature (Layne and colleagues on disenfranchised grief). Pet loss has its own (Doka on loss types). Anticipatory loss while caregiving has its own (Boss on ambiguous loss). Support context varies: someone with attentive friends has different needs from someone whose social network has gone quiet at six weeks — a documented pattern in grief social-support research. Age, relationship type, and the suddenness of the loss all shift what serves the bereaved person. (Inferred) Any direction that treats grief as a single audience will serve the median experience and miss the population edges where underservice is most acute.

The strategic positioning, then, sits between transactional logistics tools and social/community tools — a space where current offerings are sparse and where the academic and practitioner literature points toward unmet need. What's open is which direction inside that positioning actually reaches the audience.
