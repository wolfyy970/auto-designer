Users in this product space are navigating grief weeks to months after a meaningful loss — past the acute logistics phase but early enough that grief shapes their daily experience. The losses vary significantly (parent, partner, friend, pregnancy, pet, anticipatory loss from terminal diagnosis), and support systems differ enormously. This audience has told us in interviews that existing tools fail them: funeral sites are transactional and pre-focused, peer communities feel too exposed or noisy, and journaling apps with generic mood tags offer nothing substantive. They want to be treated as someone grieving, not a user to optimize through a funnel or feed.

Privacy emerges as a critical concern across every conversation we've had. People explicitly do not want grief-related content appearing in social feeds, search history, or shared accounts. This shapes not just feature decisions but fundamental assumptions about data handling, authentication, and social graph integration. (Inferred) Any design that requires social sharing, public visibility, or loosely scoped data retention will likely face adoption resistance from this audience.

The current market offers three rough categories, each with a structural gap. Funeral logistics sites serve a narrow pre-funeral window and stay transactional. Peer support communities and subreddits work for some but expose users to unpredictability and judgment. Journaling apps that bolt on a "feeling sad" mood option feel hollow — the user knows the app doesn't meaningfully understand what they're going through. (Inferred) This positions a meaningful opportunity: a tool that respects the gravity of the experience without prescribing a process or pretending a mood tag equals support.

Users consistently describe two failure modes in existing tools: being too prescriptive ("step 4 of 7: write a letter") and being too generic ("how are you feeling today?"). Both signal that the tool doesn't understand their specific journey. (Inferred) The design should avoid funnel logic entirely — no onboarding sequences that assume a linear path, no prompts that assume the user wants to be "guided" through grief. Instead, the experience should feel like a quiet, reliable space that adapts to where the user actually is.

We haven't committed to a single surface, a short flow, or a standalone product — that's part of what this exploration should clarify. What we know is that the opportunity is real, the audience is underserved, and the strategic direction should represent a distinct bet rather than a convergent variant of existing approaches. The research points toward an experience rooted in privacy, non-prescriptive interaction, and genuine respect for the user's grief rather than a tool designed to move them toward a defined outcome.
