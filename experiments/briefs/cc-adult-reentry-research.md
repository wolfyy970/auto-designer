The Community College Survey of Student Engagement (CCCSE) — including its Survey of Entering Student Engagement (SENSE) — is the primary research instrument tracking community college student experiences in the United States. CCCSE research consistently identifies barriers for community-college students: lack of information about early processes (registration, orientation, payment), institutional debts, textbook costs, and unmet financial needs blocking persistence. More than half of community college students are now adults aged 25 and older; state "reconnect" programs increasingly target adult learners specifically.

The "Five Ps of the Adult Learner Journey" conceptual framework (Innovative Higher Education, 2024) and the OCCRL Adult Learner Guidebook describe the structural differences between adult and traditional-aged community college students. Adult learners typically balance work, caregiving, and education; have less institutional knowledge from a prior school experience that may be decades old; face technology and pedagogy that's changed since they last attended; and arrive with varied prior credit (some transferable, some not). Their decision to re-enroll is often urgent (industry shift, job loss, credential needed for promotion) rather than developmental.

The user is the re-entering adult learner. They are 25, 35, 45, 55. They may be a single parent, an immigrant pursuing US credentials, a worker laid off in an industry transition, a retiree adding a credential, a person whose first college attempt was interrupted. They have signed up and are heading toward the first day. (Inferred from the consistency of "I didn't know who to ask" patterns in CCCSE research) the gap between enrolled and persisting is shaped less by academic ability than by navigation friction.

The institutional context is shaped by community-college budget realities: advising loads can be 800:1 or higher; advisors respond to email in a multi-day window; orientation is built for fall-cohort 18-year-olds. The financial-aid office, the bursar, the testing center, the disability-services office, and the registrar are typically separate offices with their own hours and processes. Adult learners with limited time often cannot navigate this on the institution's schedule.
