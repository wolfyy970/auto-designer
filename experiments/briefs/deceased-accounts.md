# Closing a deceased family member's accounts — design exploration

When a person dies, someone in their family inherits an administrative ordeal that no one is trained for. They are typically the spouse, an adult child, or a sibling — designated as the personal representative or executor — and they suddenly need to identify, contact, and close out dozens of accounts: bank accounts, brokerage accounts, credit cards, utilities, subscriptions, mortgages, retirement accounts, insurance policies, social-security and government benefits, and the deceased's digital life (email, photos, social-media accounts). Each institution has its own forms, its own definitions, its own staff. Debt collectors — sometimes within days — start contacting the family. The CFPB notes that survivors are often falsely led to believe they are personally responsible for the deceased's debts; they are not, in most cases, but the conversation is intimidating and the rules vary by state.

The user is grieving. They are the personal representative because someone had to be. They are doing this work in the evenings, between work meetings, between the funeral and the rest of life. They may live in a different state than the deceased. They have a death certificate, a stack of un-opened mail, a phone full of voicemails from creditors, and access to a few accounts but not most. The process unfolds over months. Existing resources (the CFPB's "managing someone else's money" series, attorney guides, online checklists) are accurate but encyclopedic — they list 80 things the executor might need to do, with no way to know which 12 apply today.

We are exploring what a tool for this experience could be. The scope is the full work of closing accounts and settling the estate — from the first week after death through the final dissolution months later. The shape of the solution is open: a guided web tool, a paper workbook the funeral home hands the family, a phone-based assistant, a service that does the work for the family with their authorization, or something else. What we want are distinct strategic directions that take seriously both the grief and the bureaucracy.
