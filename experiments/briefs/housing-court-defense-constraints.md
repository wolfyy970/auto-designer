Unauthorized practice of law (UPL) is a hard legal constraint. A non-attorney tool cannot give individualized legal advice on specific facts in most jurisdictions. The design must navigate the line between "legal information" (allowed) and "legal advice" (not allowed without licensure), which varies state-by-state. Document-preparation services in many jurisdictions have explicit exceptions; the design must operate within them.

Housing court procedure varies materially by jurisdiction. Notice requirements, defense deadlines, hearing formats, fee waivers, and appeal rights are all state-by-state and sometimes city-by-city. The design must hold this variance correctly.

The audience includes tenants with limited English proficiency, tenants with disabilities, tenants with low literacy. WCAG 2.2 AA accessibility is a floor; multilingual support (at minimum Spanish for US implementation) is generally required. Audio and read-aloud are likely necessary for low-literacy users; the tenant cannot afford to misunderstand the question.

Confidentiality matters. The tenant's communications with a tool that operates in the legal space may or may not be privileged depending on architecture and jurisdiction. The design must be transparent about whether records could be subpoenaed and provide appropriate caution. (Inferred) data-minimization and end-to-end encryption are likely requirements.

Court-electronic-filing (e-filing) systems vary; some courts only accept paper. The design must produce both formats where needed and provide clear instructions for the tenant on how to physically file when e-filing is unavailable.

The user is often making decisions about housing, work, and family within the same window. The design must respect these competing constraints — the tenant cannot pause life to focus on the eviction.
