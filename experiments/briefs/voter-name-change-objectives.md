The design should let the voter find out, for their state, whether they need to update voter registration at all, by what date, with what documentation. The design move is to make the state-by-state variance answerable inside the experience rather than requiring the voter to figure it out from a tangle of state-board pages. The failure mode to avoid: a confident single answer that papers over real state variance, where the voter follows the guidance and discovers at the polling place that it was wrong for their jurisdiction.

The design should treat the registration update as one task inside the bureaucratic cascade that follows a legal name change — driver's license, Social Security card, employer records, banking, passport, professional credentials — and meet the voter while they are already cataloging changes. The design move is to position the registration update in relation to the other tasks the voter is doing, rather than asking the voter to context-switch into voter-rolls thinking on its own terms. The failure mode to avoid: a flow that demands so much civic-system context to enter that the voter parks the task and returns to it only after the issue surfaces at the polling place.

The design should let the voter understand whether what they submitted is actually going to be accepted by their state's election authority, before election day. The design move is to surface the registrar's processing state in a way the voter can act on — what the registrar received, what documentation may still be needed, whether the update is on file. The failure mode to avoid: a confirmation screen that feels conclusive but reflects only that the form was submitted, not that it was accepted.

The design should hold the privacy of the name-change event itself. For some voters — trans voters who have left a deadname behind, survivors of domestic violence whose former name is unsafe to expose, voters whose name change is private from family or employer — the old name must not be required, displayed, or stored in ways that compromise the voter. The design move is to keep the prior name out of the surface and out of unnecessary storage. The failure mode to avoid: a privacy posture so cautious that voters who would benefit from the design seeing both names (to confirm a registration record) are unable to complete the update.

The design should sit alongside related name-change tasks without overstepping into civic territory the voter has not authorized. The design move is to coordinate visibility with adjacent tasks — DMV, SSA, employer — while keeping voter registration as a separate decision the voter is making themselves. The failure mode to avoid: a coordinated bundle that auto-files voter registration alongside DMV updates without the voter understanding that registration is now in a different state.
