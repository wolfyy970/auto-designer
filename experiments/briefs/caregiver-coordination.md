# Coordinating care for an aging parent — design exploration

An adult child is now in some way responsible for the day-to-day care of their aging parent. AARP's Caregiving in the US 2025 report places the number of family caregivers in the United States at roughly 63 million — a 45 percent increase over the past decade. Roughly one in four American adults is a caregiver. Twenty-six percent of caregivers now report difficulty coordinating care, up from 19 percent in 2015. Most caregivers interact directly with the care recipient's health-care providers — particularly family doctors (66 percent), pharmacists (52 percent), and nurse practitioners (47 percent). The caregiver becomes the system, because no other system is doing the work.

The user is the adult child. They live in a different city than their parent. They are working full-time, may be raising their own kids, and now are also managing their parent's medications, appointments, insurance disputes, in-home aide schedule, finances, and (over the longer term) decisions about home modifications, assisted living, or hospice. They are doing this often with sibling involvement that ranges from supportive to fractious. The information lives across their phone, their email, their parent's hand-written notes, the parent's doctor's portal (one per doctor, often three or four), Medicare paperwork, and the in-home aide's notebook. Coordination failure has consequences: missed medications, missed appointments, duplicated care, ED visits that could have been prevented.

We are exploring what care coordination for an aging parent could feel like when designed for the family caregiver. The scope is the ongoing work of coordinating — not a single appointment but the rhythm of a month. The shape of the solution is open: a family-coordination layer that includes siblings, an integration with the parent's PCP that surfaces the caregiver-visible information, a caregiver-side companion that buffers the parent's information for use across providers, a financial-and-medical decision-support surface, or something else. We want strategic directions.
