The design should make the difference between intensity tiers (high-dosage 1:1 multiple-times-per-week vs lighter once-weekly chain vs free peer/AI options) legible to the parent on first encounter, so the parent can see what the evidence base supports before they price-shop. The failure mode to avoid: an evidence-explainer so prominent that the parent who cannot afford or schedule the highest-intensity option walks away with no plan, when a lower-intensity option matched to their constraints would have served the child.

The design should surface the school's own intervention pathways (Title I tutoring, MTSS tiers, learning specialist, IEP supports where applicable) alongside the private-market options the parent is choosing among, so the public-resource question gets asked rather than skipped. The failure mode to avoid: a private-market-only routing that ignores entitlements the family already has, or a school-first routing so insistent that the parent in a school with thin services gets stuck in a referral loop while the child falls further behind.

The design should hold the parent's whole constraint mix (budget, schedule, child's emotional state, child's specific academic gaps) as visible inputs the match adapts to, rather than running the parent through a tutor-selection funnel that asks only about subject and price. The failure mode to avoid: an overfit configurator that asks the parent for so much detail upfront that they bail before any option surfaces, leaving them with the original tangle they came in with.

The design should treat the child as a participant whose comfort and dignity shape the match — how the framing reaches them, whether they have any say in tutor-fit, how grades and assessments are talked about in the household — rather than treating the child as a problem to be remediated. The failure mode to avoid: a tutor-match that closes the academic gap by producing a child who has internalized "I'm bad at math," which damages the longer-run outcome the design exists to support.

The design should give the parent who is not the math expert a way to recognize tutor-fit quality without having to evaluate pedagogy themselves — through what the tutor's working session actually looks like, through how the tutor interacts with the parent on initial contact, through readable signals about the tutor's training and approach. The failure mode to avoid: a credentialing display so heavy on certifications that the actually-effective tutor without the formal credential gets filtered out, or a review-aggregation surface that lets a polished marketing operation outrank a tutor whose students actually progress.
