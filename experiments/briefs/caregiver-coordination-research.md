AARP's "Caregiving in the US 2025" report and "Caregiving in the United States 2020" are the most comprehensive available datasets on family caregiving. The 2025 report places the number of family caregivers in the United States at roughly 63 million — a 45 percent increase over the past decade. Roughly one in four American adults is a family caregiver. Twenty-six percent of caregivers report difficulty coordinating care (up from 19 percent in 2015). Caregivers interact directly with the care recipient's health-care providers: 66 percent with family doctors, 52 percent with pharmacists, 47 percent with nurse practitioners.

AARP's 2017 "Designing Technology for Caregivers" report is a foundational design-research document for this audience, documenting specific needs (information access, communication, scheduling, medication management) and the failure modes of existing tools (too complex, too fragmented, focused on the care recipient instead of the caregiver, requiring uncompensated coordination work). Family Caregiver Alliance research and Home Alone Alliance research (clinical-skill-building for family caregivers) extend the picture.

The user is the family caregiver, most commonly an adult child but also spouses, siblings, grandchildren, partners, and "kin caregivers" without legal-family designation. Caregivers are increasingly multi-generational (caring for both parents and children, the "sandwich generation") and increasingly in the workforce — 61 percent of caregivers are employed alongside their caregiving. The caregiver is typically not paid, not formally trained in clinical skills despite being asked to perform them, and operating with little role-recognition from the health-care and social-service systems.

(Inferred from the consistency of AARP and FCA research and the breadth of "I do everything myself" reports) the caregiver is the de-facto coordinator across the care recipient's physicians, pharmacists, home-health workers, family members, financial institutions, insurance carriers (Medicare, Medigap, possibly Medicaid), and broader community. No existing system holds this role; the caregiver is the system.
