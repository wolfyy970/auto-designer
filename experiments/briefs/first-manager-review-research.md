SHRM, Gallup, and Lattice publish convergent research on the performance-review failure mode. Gallup's "More Harm Than Good" framing — that traditional performance reviews and approaches to feedback are often so bad they make performance worse about one-third of the time — has been widely cited. Lattice's research on the neuroscience of receiving feedback documents that both reviewer and reviewee are typically highly anxious, often selectively listening, often "not at their best." SHRM has documented that most people managers are not trained to assess employees and many do not have time for the conversation the conversation deserves.

First-time managers face additional difficulty. They have typically been promoted from individual contributor based on technical excellence, with little formal training in management, and are now responsible for writing reviews of people they may have worked with as peers six months earlier. They face HR-driven calibration cycles where their ratings will be compared with other managers' ratings, often without clear shared standards. They are anxious about ratings that feel unkind and ratings that feel unfounded.

The user is the first-time manager mid-review-cycle. They have a stack of notes (some in Notion, some in Slack DMs, some in their own head), a template handed down by HR with 5-15 sections, a deadline that is approaching, and the cursor sitting on a blank page. Their relationship with the employee being reviewed is sometimes the relationship that brought them into management (they hired or championed the person), sometimes inherited, sometimes complicated by a peer-to-manager transition.

(Inferred from the consistency of practitioner-blog patterns) the writing of the review is the moment many managers wish they had been writing different notes all along. The notes that exist are insufficient; the manager is forced to either reconstruct from memory or generalize in ways that produce vague feedback the employee experiences as unhelpful or unfair.
