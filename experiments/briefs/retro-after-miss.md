# A sprint retro after missing the target — design exploration

A team has just finished a two-week sprint and missed what they committed to. The scrum master has 45 minutes booked for a retrospective on Friday afternoon. The team is tired, frustrated, and uneven in how comfortable they are talking honestly about what went wrong. Atlassian and Scrum.org documentation is direct about the failure mode: without psychological safety, the retro becomes an endless cycle of blame and finger-pointing rather than a real reflection. Industry research (McKinsey, cited by Atlassian) suggests teams that regularly hold real retrospectives are roughly 14 percent more likely to hit project goals than teams that don't — but only when the retro is honest, not performative.

The user is the scrum master or facilitating engineering manager. They want a real conversation about what went wrong and why; they want the team to leave with action items they will actually try; they do not want the conversation to become a public airing of grievances against one engineer, or a defensive performance for the PM in the room, or 45 minutes of saying "yeah it was tough." Existing retro tools (Miro templates, FunRetro / EasyRetro, Jira retro modules) are useful for capturing notes but not for the harder work of getting the conversation to honesty — especially when leadership is in the room.

We are exploring what a sprint retro could feel like, particularly after a missed target. The scope is the retrospective meeting itself plus the prep and follow-through. The shape of the solution is open: an asynchronous-first retro flow that surfaces individual reflection before the meeting, a facilitation-assistant for the scrum master, an AI-assisted pattern-detector across past retros that points at what's actually changing, a structured psychological-safety scaffold, or something else. We want strategic directions.
