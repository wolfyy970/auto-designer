The patient population skews older, with higher rates of vision impairment, hearing loss, mild cognitive impairment, and reduced fine-motor dexterity than the general population. Type sizes must be at least 14pt (preferably 16pt) for printed materials; digital surfaces must support OS-level text scaling, screen readers, and high-contrast modes. Touch targets on any handheld device must follow established guidance (Apple HIG, Material Design) at the larger end of the recommended range. WCAG 2.2 AA compliance is a floor, not a ceiling, for this audience.

Health literacy varies widely; the median is below the level at which standard discharge instructions are typically written. Plain-language guidelines from the Plain Language Action and Information Network (PLAIN) and CDC's Clear Communication Index should govern copy. Medical terminology must be paired with everyday equivalents on first reference; sentences should be short; key actions should be visually distinct from explanatory text.

The hospital is a HIPAA-covered entity, and any patient-facing surface that transmits PHI must be HIPAA-compliant. SMS and unencrypted email cannot carry PHI by default. If the design moves outside the hospital's clinical systems (a patient-facing app, a SMS companion), the architecture must address authentication, consent, and BAA-covered data flow with the hospital and any other entities. This is a hard constraint.

Discharge medication reconciliation has documented error rates that are clinically significant. Any design that surfaces medication lists or schedules must either pull from the verified medication-reconciliation record (not the patient's own recollection) or be explicit that the patient should not rely on it for what to take. Inaccurate medication information has direct patient-safety consequences; we cannot accept an architecture that risks producing or amplifying medication errors.

The patient's home environment varies dramatically — Wi-Fi may or may not exist, support people may or may not be present, transportation may or may not be available, the home itself may or may not be safe for the post-discharge state. The design must degrade gracefully: any feature that requires reliable internet must have a paper or SMS or phone-call fallback; any feature that assumes a family caregiver must work for the patient alone.

The clinical content itself is owned by the care team; the design must not introduce content that contradicts or substitutes for clinical judgment. The role of the design is to mediate information transfer and adherence, not to issue clinical instructions. Clinicians must be able to override and customize at the patient level; the design cannot become an obstacle to clinical judgment in cases that don't fit the template.
