Nava's published research from partnership work with state labor agencies — most prominently the California Employment Development Department during the COVID-19 unemployment-claim surge — documents the user side of filing for unemployment insurance. In one cohort, 88 percent of research participants did not understand what an EDDCAN (a unique customer number) was or where to find it; Nava's recommendation to replace that requirement with a date-of-birth identifier was based on direct observation of users failing the existing flow. Across Nava's broader insights, claimants from low-income or variable-schedule jobs face additional barriers: their work histories don't fit the forms cleanly, their schedules don't accommodate the wait, and they distrust the systems they're forced to use.

Unemployment insurance is administered as a federal-state partnership. Each state runs its own program with its own application, its own eligibility rules, its own portal. Federal rules set floors (the kinds of work covered, the maximum benefit duration in normal times, the appeal rights) but the user experience varies wildly: some state portals are decades old, some are recent rebuilds, some are paper-only. (Inferred from the consistency of pandemic-era documented failures) the experience-quality gap between best-in-class and worst-in-class state portals is the largest single determinant of whether eligible workers can actually file.

The user is recently unemployed. The job loss itself is the precipitating event, but the cognitive and emotional state varies — a planned layoff with severance feels different than a sudden firing or hour-cut; a person with savings is making different decisions than someone with two weeks of rent left. Most claimants are filing on a phone, often during inconvenient hours, often with intermittent connectivity. They face a fraud-control regime layered on top of the application — identity-proofing (NIST 800-63 levels), reCAPTCHA challenges, document uploads — designed to defend against an organized-fraud problem the claimant did not create and is bearing the burden of.

Post-submission, claim status is the second pain point. Claimants need to know whether their claim is in process, what's needed from them next, and when the first payment will arrive. The current state in most jurisdictions is opaque: paper letters that arrive weeks later, portals that show stale information, phone lines with hold times measured in hours. (Inferred from documented patterns) the absence of trustworthy status information is the most damaging part of the post-filing experience.
