"Small-business owner" in U.S. tax terms is mostly a sole proprietor or single-member LLC filing a Schedule C, not a payroll-having S-corp. The IRS's Statistics of Income data on nonfarm sole proprietorships shows tens of millions of Schedule C filings annually, with the majority reporting modest gross receipts — these are freelancers, gig workers, side-business operators, single-shingle service providers, and small trade owners, not the firms with bookkeepers that small-business tax software marketing tends to depict. The IRS Taxpayer Burden surveys (and the National Taxpayer Advocate's Annual Reports to Congress) consistently identify self-employed filers as carrying the highest per-return compliance burden of any individual-filer category, measured in both hours and out-of-pocket cost. (Inferred) The relevant audience for a "help with taxes" product is dominated by people whose tax situation is genuinely more complex than a W-2 filer's but who do not have the revenue, the staff, or the inclination to retain a year-round bookkeeper or CPA.

The current landscape sorts into four rough categories, each with documented failure modes for this audience. Branded consumer software (TurboTax Self-Employed, H&R Block Self-Employed, TaxSlayer, FreeTaxUSA) walks filers through a Schedule C interview but charges substantially more than its W-2 equivalent and has been the subject of sustained investigative reporting (ProPublica's multi-year series by Elliott and Kiel) and federal enforcement (the FTC's 2024 final order against Intuit for deceptive "free" advertising) over upsell and dark-pattern practices. Paid preparers — storefront chains and independent enrolled agents and CPAs — are the most common alternative; GAO and NACAA investigations have repeatedly documented wide quality variance among unregulated preparers, including significant error rates in mystery-shopper audits. IRS Free File and the new IRS Direct File pilot are largely scoped to simpler W-2 situations and have historically excluded most Schedule C filers; GAO has reported low utilization of Free File relative to eligibility. Bookkeeping-first tools (QuickBooks Self-Employed, Wave, Bench, FreshBooks) address the year-round categorization problem that feeds tax prep but generally hand filers back to one of the first three categories at filing time. (Inferred) Across all four, the structural gap is that the filer still ends up reconciling a year of unsorted transactions, untracked mileage, and uncertain deductions in a short window before the deadline.

The behavioral context around small-business tax prep is shaped by the year-round-vs-annual mismatch and by quarterly-estimated-tax obligations that most Schedule C filers are statutorily subject to but routinely under-comply with — a pattern the IRS has flagged repeatedly and that the tax-gap literature (Slemrod and colleagues) treats as a major component of individual-income-tax noncompliance. The work of "doing taxes" for this audience is rarely a single April sitting; it is the accumulated cost of a year of unmade categorization decisions, missed receipts, and unrecorded mileage, surfaced under deadline pressure. Pew Research's work on small-business and gig-worker financial behavior and the JPMorgan Chase Institute's research on income volatility among independent earners both document the cash-flow irregularity that makes setting aside estimated payments difficult in practice. (Inferred) The emotional load the brief alludes to ("hate") is plausibly less about the filing UI itself than about the dread of confronting a year of unfinished bookkeeping under time pressure with money owed.

The audience varies along axes that filter direction-fit sharply. Filing complexity differs by an order of magnitude between a one-person service business with a handful of 1099s and a multi-state filer with inventory, contractors, home-office allocation, and vehicle depreciation. Bookkeeping maturity ranges from "shoebox of receipts" to "categorized spreadsheet" to "live accounting software" — and the user research literature on small-business owners' financial-tool adoption (NFIB and Federal Reserve Small Business Credit Survey data) shows the lower end is more common than the software-marketing depiction suggests. Tax literacy is uneven, with deduction confusion and self-employment-tax mechanics consistently named in IRS taxpayer-experience research as confusion points. (Inferred) A direction calibrated to the well-bookkept, software-adopting tail of the population will miss the median Schedule C filer the brief is plausibly pointing at; the inverse is also true.

The strategic positioning, then, sits in the space between year-round bookkeeping tools and once-a-year filing software — a space where current offerings either assume bookkeeping discipline the filer doesn't have, charge upsell-heavy prices for a Schedule C interview, or hand the filer to a preparer market of uneven quality. The literature points toward unmet need on the categorization-and-confidence side of the workflow, well upstream of the filing UI itself. What's open is which direction inside that positioning actually meets a self-employed filer where they are when April approaches.
