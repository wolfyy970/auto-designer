"Small-business owner" is not a single audience. The IRS Statistics of Income data and SBA's annual *Small Business Profile* together describe a population of roughly 33 million U.S. small businesses, of which the overwhelming majority — about 27 million — are nonemployer firms (sole proprietors, single-member LLCs, gig-economy 1099 workers) with no payroll and often no separate books ([SBA Office of Advocacy, *Small Business Profile*](https://advocacy.sba.gov/category/research/small-business-profiles/)). The remaining several million are employer firms with one to roughly five hundred employees, filing as S-corporations, partnerships, or C-corporations. The tax obligations, document burden, and adviser relationships at these two ends of the distribution are essentially different problems. The Schedule C sole proprietor with $40,000 of gross receipts and a shoebox of receipts is in a different situation from the ten-person S-corp paying quarterly estimated taxes, running payroll, and reconciling a separate business bank account. Any product that treats "small-business owner" as one persona will fit neither well.

Within that population, the unmet-need concentration is at the lower end. JPMorgan Chase Institute's *Small Business Cash Flow* research finds that the median small business holds about 27 days of cash buffer, with the bottom quartile holding fewer than 13 days ([JPMC Institute](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/business-growth-and-entrepreneurship)). Pew's *American Trends Panel* and NFIB's quarterly *Small Business Economic Trends* surveys consistently rank taxes among the top three concerns for owners of nonemployer and microemployer firms, alongside cost of insurance and finding customers ([NFIB](https://www.nfib.com/surveys/small-business-economic-trends/)). The IRS's *Taxpayer Compliance Burden* studies estimate that small-business taxpayers spend an average of more than 80 hours per year on federal tax compliance, with self-employed filers reporting both the highest time burden per dollar of revenue and the highest out-of-pocket spending on preparers and software ([Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration reports](https://www.tigta.gov/)). The pain is concentrated where the resources are thinnest.

The current tooling landscape splits into four bands. (1) Consumer DIY tax software extended into self-employment: TurboTax Self-Employed, H&R Block Self-Employed, TaxAct, FreeTaxUSA. These products are optimized for one annual event — filing the return — and treat year-round bookkeeping as out of scope, surfacing it only as the painful intake at January when the user must reconstruct the year from credit-card statements. (2) Year-round bookkeeping software with tax features bolted on: QuickBooks Self-Employed and the larger QuickBooks Online, FreshBooks, Wave, Xero. These products track transactions throughout the year but require ongoing categorization discipline from the owner, and the tax-time export typically still needs a human (CPA, EA, or DIY filer) to translate into a return. (3) "Done-for-you" bookkeeping-plus-tax services: Bench, Pilot, 1-800Accountant, Block Advisors, Collective (for S-corp owners specifically). These services take the bookkeeping off the owner's plate in exchange for a monthly fee, and bundle annual filing. (4) The traditional adviser channel: CPAs, Enrolled Agents, and unenrolled preparers, ranging from neighborhood storefronts to the seasonal H&R Block office to specialized small-business firms. The National Society of Accountants' *Income & Fees Survey* documents typical fees of $300–$600 for a Schedule C filing and $800–$1,500 for an S-corp or partnership return, with significant regional variation ([NSA](https://www.nsacct.org/)).

The failure modes are documented and consistent across these bands. Consumer DIY software fails when the owner reaches a question the software cannot disambiguate — vehicle expenses for a vehicle used partly for business, home-office deductions in a year of mixed remote/in-person work, depreciation of equipment, the qualified business income deduction phase-outs, payments to 1099 contractors that may or may not have triggered a 1099-NEC filing obligation. The software's interview pattern presents a single linear path through the return; when the user gets stuck on one branch, there is no expert in the loop to confirm the answer, and the burden of correctness silently transfers to the user, who is the one signing the return under penalty of perjury. Year-round bookkeeping software fails earlier in the cycle: the National Federation of Independent Business and academic studies of small-business financial management consistently document that the largest single source of tax-time pain is missing or misclassified transactions, especially for owners who commingle personal and business spending on the same card. The transaction-categorization UI in QuickBooks-class products is a daily-discipline product being used by people who do not have daily discipline available. (Inferred from the volume of "shoebox-to-Schedule-C" reconstruction services advertised every January–April) the most common state the system is in by year-end is "not maintained."

Done-for-you services solve the discipline problem but introduce a new failure mode: trust and continuity. Bench's 2024 shutdown left an estimated 12,000+ small-business customers needing to reconstruct books mid-cycle and find a new provider, which exposed the structural concentration risk of outsourcing the books to a single vendor ([reporting in *TechCrunch*, *Axios*, and *Accounting Today*, December 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/27/online-bookkeeping-startup-bench-suddenly-shuts-down/)). Beyond that specific event, owner reviews and small-business forums (r/smallbusiness, r/tax, the Indie Hackers and Modern CPA Success Show discussions) describe a recurring pattern: monthly bookkeeping is delivered late, the bookkeeper is offshore and reachable only by email with multi-day turnarounds, the year-end tax filer is a different person than the monthly bookkeeper and questions reveal that nobody owns the whole picture, and pricing creeps each year. The traditional adviser channel — the local CPA the owner has used for ten years — solves the trust and continuity problem but is in supply crisis. AICPA workforce data and *Journal of Accountancy* reporting describe a roughly 17 percent decline in accounting-program graduates over the past decade and an aging practitioner population; small-business CPAs are increasingly refusing new clients or capping at the high end of the revenue range, leaving the under-$500K-revenue owner with fewer human options each year ([AICPA *Trends Report*](https://us.aicpa.org/becomeacpa/thispathforward/trendsreport)).

The audience varies along several axes that filter direction-fit. *Entity type* (Schedule C sole prop vs. single-member LLC vs. S-corp vs. partnership vs. C-corp) determines the form set, the deadlines (March 15 for pass-throughs, April 15 for sole props and C-corps), the payroll obligation, and whether the owner pays self-employment tax on the full profit or only on reasonable compensation. *Industry* shapes which deductions are common and audit-flag-prone — vehicle and meals for sales-driven businesses, cost of goods sold for product businesses, home office and equipment for solo professional services, inventory accounting for retailers, sales tax in addition to income tax for anyone selling taxable goods or services. *State* layers state income tax (in 41 states), local business taxes, state-specific sales tax registration, and in some states gross-receipts taxes (Washington's B&O, Ohio's CAT, Texas's franchise tax) that have no federal analogue. *Multi-state exposure* is increasingly common after Wayfair (2018) and remote-work normalization — an owner with one location can now have economic nexus in a dozen states for sales tax, and remote employees can create payroll and income-tax filing obligations in their states of residence. *Language and immigration status* matter materially for the long tail of immigrant-owned microbusinesses; ITIN filers and mixed-status families weigh tax compliance against immigration risk, especially in current enforcement environments. *Adviser literacy* varies: some owners can read a P&L and a balance sheet, many cannot, and the gap is a strong predictor of how a product's outputs need to be framed.

The lived experience, as documented in small-business owner interviews, podcasts, and survey free-response data, is dominated by anxiety and avoidance. Owners describe putting off tax work because every step reveals another step they didn't know about — the realization that 1099-NECs needed to be filed by January 31 for contractors paid the prior year, the discovery that quarterly estimated taxes were owed and the underpayment penalty is now accruing, the recognition that the home-office deduction requires square-footage and exclusive-use documentation they don't have. The deadline pressure is bimodal: the heaviest week is the last week of March (S-corp/partnership deadline) and the last week of two weeks before April 15 (when the consumer-software experience degrades under load and CPAs stop accepting new returns). Extensions are widely used — the IRS *Filing Season Statistics* show roughly 20 million Form 4868 extensions filed annually — but extensions only delay the filing, not the payment, which is a distinction many owners learn the hard way. (Inferred from the dominance of "ugh," "dread," and "overwhelm" language in owner-facing forums during Q1) the emotional frame of the work, more than the cognitive complexity, is what drives avoidance and late filing.

The strategic positioning gap sits between three things the existing landscape does not connect well: (a) the *year-round* transaction-and-document substrate, which exists in bookkeeping tools but requires discipline most owners cannot sustain; (b) the *moment of truth* annual filing, which exists in tax software but treats the rest of the year as someone else's problem; and (c) the *trusted human* who can answer the question that doesn't fit the software's interview pattern, which exists in the CPA channel but is increasingly unavailable to the under-$500K-revenue tier and is structurally hard to scale. The shutdowns of done-for-you services, the supply crisis in the CPA pipeline, and the bookkeeping-discipline failure mode together suggest that the underserved territory is the small-business owner who wants a single coherent thread from January 1 to April 15 of the following year, with enough human-or-human-quality judgment in the loop to handle the questions the rule-based software cannot, at a price point the under-$500K-revenue business can absorb. Whether that's a software product with embedded expert review, a service productized down-market, or something else is what the design problem will need to choose between.
