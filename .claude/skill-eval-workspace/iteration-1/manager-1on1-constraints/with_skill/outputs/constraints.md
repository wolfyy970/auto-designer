**Target surface: open.** The brief explicitly leaves surface choice as part of what design exploration should resolve — single tightly-scoped surface, short flow, or standalone product are all on the table. The strategic surface declaration is `open`, with the constraint that the surface must be reachable inside the 15–30 minute pre-meeting window the brief locates the user in: any surface that requires the user to leave the context they are already in (Slack, calendar, browser tab, notes app) and adopt a new daily-driver tool is filtered out by the operational realities below, not by the surface declaration itself.

Regulatory frameworks apply because this is an employment-context tool, and content produced or stored in 1:1 prep can become discoverable in employment disputes. Title VII, the ADA, and the ADEA all govern the employment relationship the tool sits inside; the EEOC's 2023–2024 guidance on AI in employment decisions (EEOC technical assistance on the ADA and AI, May 2022; EEOC technical assistance on Title VII and AI, May 2023) treats manager-facing tools that influence decisions as in-scope. The NLRA's Section 7 protections (NLRB on protected concerted activity) make surveillance-shaped directions — anything that aggregates what reports say in shared channels and surfaces it to the manager — legally hazardous regardless of design quality. Several states regulate employee monitoring with notice requirements (Connecticut Gen. Stat. §31-48d; Delaware Code §19-705; New York Civil Rights Law §52-bb effective 2022). For organizations with European employees, GDPR treats employment data as subject to additional safeguards (Article 88; EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 on consent in the employment context note the power imbalance that limits consent as a lawful basis). CCPA/CPRA's employee-data provisions are in force in California as of 2023. Directions that quietly normalize employer access to report-side communication, or that produce AI-generated assessments of named individuals, are not viable independent of how well they are designed.

Audience reach realities filter the exploration space at two levels. The manager population is wide: the brief locates the user in the first 6–18 months of management, but that population spans industries, company sizes, native-language differences, and disability composition no narrower than the general working-adult population (BLS Current Population Survey on management occupations; CDC disability-and-health data on prevalence of disability in working-age adults). Directions that assume a specific tech-stack fluency (the user has Notion, the user has a Jira API token, the user has admin access to their HRIS) fail to reach the audience whose underservice the brief is targeting — by definition these are people new enough to management that institutional tooling access is uneven. The audience-reach version of accessibility is real: directions that fundamentally exclude managers navigating vision, motor, or cognitive disability are not viable for an audience drawn from the general workforce. (Rendering-level accessibility specifics live in execution work.) The second level: the *reports* are not direct users but are the subjects of the content the tool produces; directions that depend on the report consenting to, installing, or interacting with the tool collapse the audience-reach to organizations that can mandate adoption — a narrower and structurally different audience than the brief describes.

Cognitive, institutional, and operational realities at the viability level. The brief locates the user precisely: 15–30 minutes before a 1:1, scrambling between Slack, calendar, and last-meeting notes, with "what should I even ask?" anxiety. (Inferred from Yerkes-Dodson on arousal-performance and from Cognitive Load Theory — Sweller — on novel-schema acquisition under time pressure) The combination of moderate-to-high arousal and a hard time-budget filters out direction shapes that require schema acquisition (learn-this-framework-first), sustained configuration, or content production at the moment of need. The brief also names an institutional reality: new managers "have enough tools already" and reject anything that "feels like work — one more system to maintain." Directions that require recurring upkeep (daily journaling, structured logging between meetings, manual tagging) are not viable for this audience-state pair regardless of how good the upkeep experience is rendered to be; the constraint is on whether ongoing maintenance can be assumed at all, not on how it would be designed. Directions that assume the manager has authority to standardize a 1:1 format across an organization are also filtered out — the audience is by definition early-tenure and operating inside someone else's culture.

Hard data and access realities. (Inferred) The "previous 1:1 notes" the brief implies as context often do not exist for a first-time manager — they may not have started taking them, or they exist in a personal notes app the tool has no access to. Directions that depend on a meaningful history of prior 1:1 content as input are not viable for the early-tenure edge of the audience the brief targets. Cross-tool data access (Slack DMs, calendar metadata, Jira/Linear activity, HRIS performance data, Git/GitHub activity) cannot be assumed: most organizations do not grant individual managers programmatic access to these systems, and the integrations that exist (Slack Enterprise APIs, Workday HRIS APIs, Microsoft Graph) are admin-installed, not manager-installed. Directions whose core mechanic depends on aggregating cross-system data about a named report are feasible only for managers whose IT department has pre-installed the integration; this is a structurally different audience from "first-time managers in their first 6–18 months." Finally, content about a named report — mental health disclosures, performance concerns, interpersonal conflict — produced or stored by the tool becomes potentially discoverable in employment disputes and potentially subject to subject-access requests under GDPR/CCPA; directions that rely on long-lived cloud storage of report-identifying content carry a class of risk that filters them out for organizations with meaningful legal exposure.
