The audience is the first-time manager 6–18 months into the role: recent enough that the work still feels unfamiliar, far enough in that the calendar is already structurally full of recurring 1:1s. CCL (Center for Creative Leadership) and DDI's longitudinal research on first-time managers consistently describes a population promoted on IC excellence, given little formal training, and dropped into a job whose core unit of work — the recurring conversation with a direct report — is one they have rarely been on the senior side of before. Gallup's State of the American Workplace and Manager Experience research has repeatedly framed the manager as the single largest variable in employee engagement, and the 1:1 as the most-cited high-leverage habit; Harvard Business Review's coverage of Julia Rozovsky's Project Aristotle synthesis and of Marcus Buckingham's "frequent light-touch check-ins" framing converge on the same point that weekly 1:1s, done with care, move the needle more than annual rituals do. The new manager knows this in the abstract and feels acute responsibility for it in the specific — which is exactly what produces the anxiety the brief describes.

The current landscape splits into three categories that fail in three different ways. (1) Agenda templates and checklists — Lighthouse's templates, the Manager Tools podcast's prescribed format, Lattice's and 15Five's downloadable PDFs, Notion 1:1 templates, Atlassian's Team Playbook entries — give structure but no situational fit; they are someone else's framework imported into a relationship that is specific, and practitioner write-ups consistently describe them being abandoned after two to six weeks once the manager has internalized the headings or decided the headings don't fit this report. (2) Team-management platforms with 1:1 modules — Lattice, 15Five, Culture Amp, Leapsome, Officevibe, Small Improvements, Friday, Range, Soapbox/Hypercontext — are built for the org rather than for the manager: they coordinate with performance reviews, OKRs, engagement surveys, and HR calibration. They are heavy, they impose schemas, and new managers describe them as compliance surfaces they update before someone in HR asks rather than tools they reach for in the fifteen minutes before a 1:1. (3) Notebooks and personal docs — a Notion page per report, Apple Notes, a running Google Doc, a paper notebook — adapt to the relationship perfectly but offer no scaffolding when the manager is staring at the page wondering what to ask the senior engineer who has gone quiet this week. Each category solves one of (structure | situational fit | personalness) and surrenders the other two.

The specific moment named in the brief — 15 to 30 minutes before the 1:1 starts, scrambling between Slack, calendar, last-meeting notes, and the "what should I even ask?" anxiety — is the moment where none of the three categories help. The first-time manager is doing parallel work in this window: reconstructing what happened since the last meeting (Slack threads they were tagged in, PRs the report opened, standup updates, the offhand comment in the team channel about workload), recalling what was promised or surfaced last time, and deciding what this meeting should actually be — a status sync, a career conversation, a check-in on something they noticed, a chance for the report to surface what's on their mind. (Inferred from the consistency of practitioner-blog and Reddit r/managers patterns) the cost of getting this moment wrong is concrete: a 1:1 that defaults to status, that the report leaves feeling unheard, that the manager leaves feeling guilty about having defaulted to status again, and that compounds into a pattern of meetings the report eventually says they want to skip.

The audience varies along axes that filter direction-fit and that the design exploration cannot ignore. The reports being managed differ — junior IC needing scaffolding, senior IC needing space, peer-turned-report carrying the awkwardness of the transition, distributed teammate whose work the manager sees mostly through artifacts, newcomer in their first ninety days needing onboarding-shaped 1:1s — and a good 1:1 with each is shaped differently. The manager's own context differs: some are in tightly instrumented orgs (Lattice plus OKRs plus weekly written check-ins) where artifacts about the report are abundant; others manage on top of Slack and calendar alone. Tooling stacks vary (Slack vs Teams; Linear vs Jira vs GitHub Projects; Notion vs Confluence vs Google Docs), and meeting cadence varies (weekly, biweekly, ad hoc). Cultural context varies — some orgs have a strong norm that the 1:1 is the report's meeting and the manager should mostly listen; others treat it as a manager-led status channel; the new manager is often reading the norm at the same time they are trying to act on it. (Inferred) Any direction that assumes a single report archetype, a single tooling stack, or a single org culture will miss the audience edges where the help is most needed.

The strategic positioning sits in the gap between the structure that templates provide rigidly and the situational fit that notebooks provide unsupported — between compliance-feeling org software and the maintenance-free help the moment actually wants. The brief explicitly frames the moment as preparation rather than capture, and that framing matters: the existing tooling category is dominated by surfaces for *during* and *after* the meeting (notes, action items, follow-up sentiment), and the *before* moment is structurally underserved. What's open for the design exploration is which direction inside that gap actually meets the manager in the fifteen-minute window without becoming the next tool they stop opening in week four.
