The user is a manager in their first 6–18 months in the role, with multiple direct reports and a recurring 1:1 cadence already established. The transition from individual contributor to manager is well documented as a discontinuous role change rather than a promotion of the same work: Linda Hill's *Becoming a Manager* (Harvard Business School Press, 2003; HBR "Becoming the Boss," 2007) describes the first managerial year as a period of identity reconstruction in which new managers consistently underestimate the people-leadership demands and overestimate how much their prior expertise transfers. Center for Creative Leadership research on leadership transitions converges on the same pattern. SHRM has repeatedly documented that the majority of new people-managers receive little or no formal training before taking on the role. Gallup's body of workplace research (most recently the *State of the Global Workplace* reports) finds that managers account for roughly 70% of the variance in team engagement, and that the frequency and quality of one-on-one conversations is among the strongest correlates of whether direct reports feel their manager cares about them as a person.

The current landscape splits roughly three ways, each with a named failure mode for this audience. Agenda-template products and checklists (Lighthouse, Hypercontext, Fellow's lighter tier, the dozens of "1:1 questions" Notion templates) provide structure but tend to be abandoned within weeks; HBR coverage of feedback and 1:1 practice consistently observes that prescriptive templates feel transactional and don't survive contact with the messy reality of a specific report. Full talent-management suites with 1:1 modules (Lattice, 15Five, CultureAmp, Leapsome) are designed around HR-administered performance cycles — useful to the org, but new managers describe them as compliance surfaces rather than thinking surfaces. Personal notebooks, Apple/Google Notes, and Notion docs work as memory aids but provide no scaffolding for the specific prep moment, which is the moment many first-time managers say they most need help. Across all three categories, MIT Sloan Management Review's coverage of manager development ("The Hard Truth About Soft Skills," and related work) flags the same gap: tools optimize for the artifact (notes, agendas, ratings) rather than for the conversational quality the artifact is supposed to enable.

Behaviorally, the prep moment is short, interstitial, and high-anxiety. The brief describes the 15–30 minutes before a 1:1 spent scrambling between Slack, calendar, and last-meeting notes; this matches a pattern visible in Gallup's manager research and in practitioner reporting (HBR's "What Great Managers Do Daily," Buckingham & Coffman's underlying *First, Break All the Rules*): new managers default to discussing status because status is what they know how to discuss, and avoid the harder conversations — career, growth, friction, signals of disengagement — that 1:1s exist for. Amy Edmondson's peer-reviewed work on psychological safety (*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 1999; synthesized in *The Fearless Organization*, 2018) supplies the foundational vocabulary for what's actually at stake in a 1:1: the conversation is a recurring micro-test of whether the direct report believes it is safe to surface a real problem to this specific manager. (Inferred) A prep tool that only helps the manager build a topic list, without supporting the relational stance the conversation actually depends on, addresses the visible scramble but leaves the underlying failure mode intact.

The audience varies along axes that filter direction-fit. Reports themselves differ substantially — a junior IC in their first 90 days, a senior IC with deep tenure, a peer recently turned report, a distributed teammate the manager only ever sees on video — and the literature on managerial relationships (Hill; Edmondson; the Gallup Q12 framing of engagement drivers) is clear that the same conversational moves do not serve all of these equally. Managers themselves vary in what they came from: technical ICs promoted for excellence, project leads who semi-formally managed before, people brought in laterally into a new team they didn't form. Org context varies: some companies wrap 1:1s in heavy HR ritual, some leave them entirely informal. (Inferred) Any direction that treats "the 1:1" as a single shape — one cadence, one report type, one manager archetype — will optimize for the median and miss the situations where the help is most needed, which tend to be precisely the non-median ones (the peer-turned-report conversation, the disengaged senior IC, the new hire whose silence is being misread as fine).

The strategic positioning sits between the agenda-template surface (too thin, gets abandoned) and the HR-suite surface (too heavy, lives in someone else's workflow). What the academic and practitioner literature points to as underserved is the *prep moment itself*: the short window where a new manager is trying to convert scattered context (last meeting's notes, this week's Slack threads, a vague sense that something is off with one report) into a stance they can walk into the conversation with. What's open is which direction inside that positioning meets the new manager at the specificity of their actual situation without becoming another system to maintain.
