Users in this product space are critical-care nurses at the moment of shift change, transferring responsibility for high-acuity patients between an outgoing and oncoming nurse. Nursing handoff has been a Joint Commission National Patient Safety Goal since 2006, and the AHRQ Patient Safety Network frames handoff as a clustering point for adverse events; the Joint Commission's 2024 Sentinel Event Annual Review continues to flag inadequate staff-to-staff communication during handoffs and transitions as a contributing factor across event types. Benner's novice-to-expert model and the body of follow-on work on "nurse intuition" describe a tacit-knowledge layer to ICU practice — expert nurses recognize patient deterioration through subtle, often pre-verbal cues ("not looking right," restlessness, skin tone) before vital signs cross thresholds, and the recently developed Nurse Intuition Patient Deterioration Scale treats this gut-feel signal as clinically meaningful in its own right. The audience-defining fact is that the handoff carries both the structured clinical state and this layer of tacit, hard-to-articulate concern at the same time.

The current landscape is a mix of structured verbal protocols and EHR-supported tools, each with documented failure modes. SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) and the more recent I-PASS bundle are the two best-evidenced structured handoff formats and improve communication completeness in pre/post studies (Starmer et al. on I-PASS; the AHRQ PSNet evidence summaries on SBAR), but ICU-specific work on I-PASS finds workload itself is a major adherence barrier, with sicker, busier units scoring lower on protocol fidelity than less acute settings. EHR-supported handoff has its own well-documented failure pattern: nurse-facing summaries are described in the literature as "too generic," poorly co-designed with nursing workflow, and a source of workaround behavior — one cited study found 34% of EHR-mediated communication generated a workaround. Bedside shift report, an AHRQ-endorsed practice intended to bring the patient and family into the handoff, runs into interruption rates documented at an average of two interruptions per ICU handoff (range 0–7), with patients, families, phone calls, and other staff as the named interrupters; a majority of nurses in qualitative work prefer a *modified* bedside report, splitting the exchange between inside and outside the room, precisely so the sensitive and tacit material has somewhere to go.

The audience varies in ways that change which directions will fit. Unit type matters: a medical ICU, surgical ICU, neuro ICU, and CCU run different patient mixes, different anticipated-decision horizons, and different family-presence patterns, and the I-PASS implementation literature shows protocol fidelity itself varies by workload between these settings. Experience level matters: Benner's framework and the follow-on intuition literature describe expert nurses carrying a substantially different kind of handoff payload than novices — more pattern-recognition, more "I have a feeling about bed 4," less line-by-line recitation — and a tool that serves only the novice end of that spectrum will be experienced by experts as documentation burden. Family and patient presence at the bedside varies bed-to-bed within a single shift, which is part of why the modified-bedside-report pattern emerged. (Inferred) These axes mean a one-shape handoff tool that works identically for every bed, every nurse, and every unit will systematically underserve the high-acuity, high-experience, family-present end of the population — which is also where the consequences of a missed handoff are largest.

The strategic positioning sits in the gap between the structured-protocol layer (SBAR, I-PASS) and the EHR-data layer — a space the published evidence describes as underserved, where the protocol carries the script and the EHR carries the data but neither carries the tacit clinical concern, the next-12-hours decision horizon, or the family/social context that nurses currently transmit through verbal-only side-channels at the bedside. The published critique of existing nurse-facing EHR handoff views is that they are designed for generic summary rather than co-designed with the bedside workflow; the published critique of verbal-only handoff is that it loses information under interruption and time pressure. The opportunity, framed without prescribing shape, is to give the tacit and decision-horizon layer a first-class place in the handoff that the protocol-and-EHR stack currently does not.
