The design should surface, on each patient's view, what is unresolved and time-bound — pending labs not yet returned, vital trends moving in a direction, decisions the team is going to face in the next several hours — so the outgoing nurse does not have to remember to mention them and the incoming nurse does not have to ask. What is stable and resolved should recede. The failure mode to avoid: a salience model so confident about what matters that it buries the one detail this particular nurse needed to flag, or surfaces so much "important" content that nothing reads as more urgent than anything else.

The per-patient view should be scannable in the seconds the verbal exchange actually has. The unit of attention is one patient for roughly twenty to thirty seconds at the bedside, and the design should let both nurses orient on that patient inside that window. The failure mode to avoid: compression that produces a checklist — bulleted state stripped of the clinical narrative that makes the state mean something — so the tool reads as the kind of reductive instrument the audience explicitly rejects.

The design should make room for the outgoing nurse's tacit reads — the "I have a feeling about bed three" that doesn't yet have a lab value behind it — as first-class content alongside the structured clinical state. There should be a place for a hunch that is not a free-text afterthought and not a forced categorization. The failure mode to avoid: a structured-input surface that flattens an experienced nurse's pattern recognition into a dropdown, or a soft-signal area so unstructured the incoming nurse cannot tell whether to weight it heavily or pass over it.

The design should compose with the bedside surfaces the nurse is already using — the scrub-pocket phone, the workstation-on-wheels with the EHR open, the verbal exchange happening at the same time — rather than competing with them for attention. A nurse who looks down at the tool should be reading something the other surfaces do not already show. The failure mode to avoid: a tool that becomes a fourth screen demanding eye time during the exchange, or one that hides so deferentially behind the existing surfaces that the nurses cannot tell what it is contributing that the EHR is not.

The outgoing nurse should remain the author of what crosses the handoff — the design supports their judgment about what to emphasize, what to soften, and what to leave for the verbal exchange, rather than auto-generating a report that the nurse then has to audit or override. The failure mode to avoid: editorial assistance so confident the nurse cannot see what was emphasized or omitted on their behalf, or so passive that the nurse is doing the documentation work the tool exists to reduce.

The handoff artifact should read symmetrically — the same object the outgoing nurse hands off is what the incoming nurse reads, without the incoming nurse having to translate from documentation shaped for the outgoing nurse's memory. The failure mode to avoid: an outgoing-side tool that the incoming nurse cannot navigate fluently, or an incoming-side view that is so reorganized for clarity that the outgoing nurse has to do extra structured-input work to feed it.
