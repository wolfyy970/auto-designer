The audience here is not "a user who forgot their password" in the abstract. It is someone whose access to money has been cut at the moment they need it — rent is due today, a transfer needs to clear, a card was declined at the register, a parent needs to confirm a balance before a kid's school deadline. The brief is explicit that the person is already stressed and needs in NOW; that pins the audience to people for whom the reset is not a chore but an obstacle between them and an unavoidable obligation. Direction shapes that assume calm, patient, "let's verify you carefully" interaction posture are clearly wrong-fit for this audience; shapes that lower cognitive load, narrate progress, and provide a visible human-shaped fallback are clearly more fit.

The audience also varies in ways that filter direction-fit. (Inferred from the brief's emphasis on stress and urgency) the population spans: people locked out because they genuinely forgot vs. people locked out because the app flagged a legitimate login as suspicious; people resetting from the same device they always use vs. from a borrowed or new one; people with the original email/phone still active vs. people whose recovery channels have lapsed (new number, lost access to old email); people whose stress is acute-financial (rent, overdraft) vs. chronic-financial (every month is this month). No single direction will serve all of these uniformly — a flow optimized for the happy "I have my phone and my email" path will fail the recovery-channel-lapsed user, and a flow that front-loads heavy identity verification will compound the stress of the user who is simply trying to pay rent.

The current landscape, described in the brief and corroborated by what is broadly known about consumer banking auth flows, fails this audience in named ways. Generic "Invalid credentials" errors collapse three distinct states (wrong password, locked account, unrecognized device) into one message that tells the user nothing actionable. Multi-step verification (email code, then SMS code, then security question, then captcha) is sequenced without acknowledging that each step is a new opportunity to fail and a new occasion for the user's stress to compound. There is typically no visible escalation path: when the automated reset fails — code didn't arrive, security answer doesn't match the one on file, recovery email is the old one — the user is left re-trying the same loop rather than offered a human or alternate-channel route. The CFPB has repeatedly documented consumer-finance access friction as a driver of complaints about banks and credit unions; the specific shape "I could not get into my own account in time" is a recurring grievance pattern, not a niche edge case.

Behaviorally and situationally, the moment matters. The reset is rarely the user's primary task — it is a blocker in front of a primary task (pay, check, transfer, confirm). The user is often on mobile, often in a non-ideal context (in line at a store, in a car, at a kitchen table the night a bill is due), and often switching between the bank app, an email client, and an SMS app to ferry codes. (Inferred from the brief's "stressed and needs in NOW" framing) the dominant emotional texture is some mix of urgency, mild shame ("how did I let this happen again"), and suspicion that the system is treating them as a fraud suspect rather than a customer. Research on scarcity and cognitive load (Mullainathan and Shafir's work on scarcity is the relevant canon here) supports the general claim that financial stress narrows attentional bandwidth — users in this state have less working memory to spend on multi-step recovery, parse error copy less charitably, and abandon flows that would be tolerable in calm conditions. The framework supports the directional claim that load-reduction matters; it does not specify a particular number of steps or a particular copy pattern, and those execution choices belong downstream.

The strategic opportunity is the gap between what today's reset flow assumes about the user (calm, patient, indifferent to which step they're on, willing to re-try the loop) and who the user actually is in the moment of reset (stressed, time-pressured, oscillating between the bank's surfaces and other apps, primed to read ambiguous system behavior as accusatory). Closing that gap means a reset experience that treats the stress as the design constraint rather than a footnote: clear about state, honest about what failed and what's next, and visibly escalable to a human or an alternate route the moment the automated path stops working for this particular user. The brief's framing — including pre/post-flow surfaces in scope — opens the door to grounding the reset in the larger access moment rather than as an isolated form, which is where the strategic leverage is.
